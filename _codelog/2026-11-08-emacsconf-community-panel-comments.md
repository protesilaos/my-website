The following is my recollection of comments I made during the
recording of the EmacsConf 2026 community panel (the Q&A session will
happen live during the conference): <https://emacsconf.org/2026/talks/community/>.

It is not a transcript though it is true in spirit.

We had 1 minute per answer.

## Starting with Emacs

> 1 What made you start using Emacs, and what kept you using it?

It is the potential of interconnectedness. With Emacs I can draw
linkages between different parts of my workflow, such as from the
email client or RSS feed reader to the agenda. It also is the
transferability of skills: when I write a custom function that works,
say, for programming then that same function can be used while writing
emails (e.g. a "focus mode" that also enlarges the font size and
switches the cursor from a box to a bar shape). Software freedom is
another important consideration because it empowers me to do my
personal computing as I want and Emacs is by far the best in this
regard.

## Contributing to Emacs

> 2 What took you from using Emacs to actively contributing to the
>   community - participating in forums, writing Elisp, organizing
>   meetups, answering questions?

One is organic. By using Emacs, I configure it by programming it. The
more I do that, the more I learn how to write Emacs Lisp. I eventually
reach a point where I can produce patches for existing libraries and
also develop my own packages. This organic growth blurs the lines
between user and developer as well as passive user and active
contributor. The other part is a sense of stewardship. I am inspired
to give back to the commons. I recognise that I benefit from the
freedom I receive and thus assume the duty of empowering others to do
the same.

## Emacs in daily life

> 3 How does Emacs show up for you in real life - colleagues,
>   meetups, friends? How did those connections form?

I talk to Emacs users on video calls. I have even met a few in person
who wanted to visit me for other reasons as well (one case was a funny
story about "a Greek philosopher who is actually alive"). Those
connections form naturally over time. I find that there is concordance
of spirit. People who use Emacs have some character traits in common.
I can see a little bit of myself reflected in them and a little bit of
them reflected in me. Each person has their individuality. Though we
all answer a certain call that compels us to explore the depths and
the lengths.

## Learning from other communities

> 4 If you know other software communities, what's one thing Emacs
>   could learn from them?

I think this is not specific to software. What is true for the Emacs
community is true for communities in general. It is about the
potential for participation. People must feel that they are part of
something greater than themselves; something that will be there even
after they are gone. Thriving communities create involvement through a
variety of means and thus engender the belief that one can move from
the notional periphery to the centre, from observer to member.
Concretely, livestreams, bug squashing events, social gatherings,
virtual or in-person conferences, and even face-to-face meetings, all
help create a sense of belonging and togetherness.

## What to change for next year

> 5 What's one concrete thing you wish existed in the Emacs community
>   a year from now?

Uphold the standard of excellence. Keep doing what we do and increase
it in intensity and/or frequency wherever possible. Those who chase
fads have a spectacular rise followed by an equally spectacular fall.
What has made Emacs---and communities in general---stand the test of
time is unflinching commitment to their core tenets. Those are the
qualities that made them interesting in the first place. In practice,
this is about focusing on quality and letting the quantity come about
organically. People come and go. To prioritise quantity is to attract
opportunists and, in the process, to erode the soil we stand on.
