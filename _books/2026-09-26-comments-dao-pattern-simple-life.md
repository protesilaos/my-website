## On pattern and patterns

From <https://www.wendangwang.com/doc/e9374217c57a304ef32b37fc1fa3883371076c9e>.

    The Tao that can be told of
    Is not the Absolute Tao;
    The Names that can be given
    Are not Absolute Names.
    The Nameless is the origin of Heaven and Earth;
    The Named is the Mother of All Things.

    Therefore:
    Oftentimes, one strips oneself of passion
    In order to see the Secret of Life;
    Oftentimes, one regards life with passion,
    In order to see its manifest forms.
    These two (the Secret and its manifestations)
    Are (in their nature) the same;
    They are given different names
    When they become manifest.
    They may both be called the Cosmic Mystery:
    Reaching from the Mystery into the Deeper Mystery
    Is the Gate to the Secret of All Life.

Below I provide my commentary by rephrasing and then explaining these
statements.

    [ORIGINAL]
    The Tao that can be told of
    Is not the Absolute Tao;
    The Names that can be given
    Are not Absolute Names.

    [PROTESILAOS]
    The Being that can be described
    is not the absolute Being;
    The names (i.e. descriptions) that can be given
    are not the absolute name.

An abstraction is the pattern common to presences. For example, "dog"
is a pattern (or set thereof) present in American Pitbull Terrier,
German Shepherd, Labrador Retriever. Each of those breed names is an
abstraction in its own right, which is common in the particular
specimens. The most abstract, then, is that which is present in
everything.

As with the case of the "dog" abstraction, the instantiations of it
exhibit modality. They not only exist; they exist in some way.
Modality is the mode of being. Modality is about the descriptors---the
differentiators or specifiers---of the given presence.

Abstractions of abstractions necessarily have increasingly abstract
modality. If something can be described, if it has a mode of being, it
has modality: a pattern that can be discerned. The most abstract is
indescribable, strictly speaking, because it lacks modality. It cannot
have modality because that renders it describable, which would mean
that it instantiates some greater abstraction.

Consequently, when we use "Being" we are limited by our language. We
give a name to something, thus describing it. What we really mean is
"that which is most abstract". We cannot avoid the contradiction of
putting it into words yet asking the reader to grasp the concept of
the indescribable: absolute Being.

What is in our head is still an approximation, even if we do not name
it or speak of it. This is because grasping involves limiting. The
most absolute is not comprehensible. Though we can approximate it,
meaning that it does not have a pattern, but is pattern as such.

A note here on theology. Being is not some deity in the familiar
sense. It (we use "it" conventionally) is not the almighty god, or
however we think of gods. Being cannot be the absolute "good", because
that entails modality: it has a disposition of being good, meaning
that there exists a non-good and, necessarily, a greater abstraction
common to those two. If god or gods exist, if they are mighty and
good, they still are presences: instantiations of Being, else pattern.
We give them names, we describe them, and so on as patterns.

Same principle for what we might think of as duality: god and the
world, mind and body, etc.. There necessarily exists a third magnitude
that encompasses the two for them to consist as duality. In other
words, there is something common to both of them. We then notice how
the two is actually three, which constitute a one, which consists of
two.

    [ORIGINAL]
    The Nameless is the origin of Heaven and Earth;
    The Named is the Mother of All Things.

    [PROTESILAOS]
    Being is the origin of all
    Tropos is what "all" is

Continuing from the above, totality consists of presences. They are,
which means that they partake of Being. But they are in some way,
which is their modality. As such, I am introducing the Greek word for
modality because I like it more: Tropos (found in words such as trope,
tropical, psychotropic, entropy, ...). All presences partake of
Tropos, which makes them describable. They are all patterns, which
have pattern as such in common.

    [ORIGINAL]
    Oftentimes, one strips oneself of passion
    In order to see the Secret of Life;
    Oftentimes, one regards life with passion,
    In order to see its manifest forms.
    These two (the Secret and its manifestations)
    Are (in their nature) the same;
    They are given different names
    When they become manifest.
    They may both be called the Cosmic Mystery:
    Reaching from the Mystery into the Deeper Mystery
    Is the Gate to the Secret of All Life.

I think here there is no need to rephrase those statements. We start
with a theme that I have mentioned in plenty of my writings, namely,
humans are multifaceted. They have reason and emotions and they can be
spiritual yet still have needs of their body. These facets do not have
a standalone presence: they are part of the greater system which we
call "human". As such, a person cannot be purely rational or
spiritual, emotional, etc. One can only ever be a bit of everything.
Wisdom, then, consists in this recognition. So we sometimes strip away
the passions to think of Being, yet at others we rely on the passions
to conceive of Tropos in something that is close to us. The sage knows
what the balance is.

For us, Being and Tropos are indistinguishable. Tropos does not have a
standalone presence. It is a name we give for instantiations of Being.
In a sense, Being does not have a standalone presence either, because
it is the most abstract of all there is so it cannot be "alone" as
there are no "others" at that level of abstraction.

The last two statements describe how we operate as humans, discerning
patterns from the particular to arrive at abstractions, and then
discerning patterns in the abstract structure that has revealed itself
to grasp a greater abstraction. We thus proceed from particular
knowledge to what is described here as the "Secret of All Life": Being
and Tropos, Being as such, pattern.

    [ORIGINAL]
    When the people of the Earth all know beauty as beauty,
    There arises (the recognition of) ugliness.
    When the people of the Earth all know the good as good,
    There arises (the recognition of) evil.

    Therefore:
    Being and non-being interdepend in growth;
    Difficult and easy interdepend in completion;
    Long and short interdepend in contrast;
    High and low interdepend in position;
    Tones and voice interdepend in harmony;
    Front and behind interdepend in company.

    Therefore the Sage:
    Manages affairs without action;
    Preaches the doctrine without words;
    All things take their rise, but he does not turn away from them;
    He gives them life, but does not take possession of them;
    He acts, but does not appropriate;

The part before the "Therefore the Sage" tells us how opposites are
not truly different and how all is one. I explained my position by
interpreting the Yin and Yang symbol:
<https://protesilaos.com/commentary/2022-07-15-yin-yang-interpretation/>.

## Wisdom and indeterminacy

What I have not covered yet is this:

    [ORIGINAL]
    Therefore the Sage:
    Manages affairs without action;
    Preaches the doctrine without words;
    All things take their rise, but he does not turn away from them;
    He gives them life, but does not take possession of them;
    He acts, but does not appropriate;

The sage can manage affairs without action because the sage does not
have to act in order to figure out what needs to be done. The sage
knows, by understanding Being and Tropos, that nothing has to be done.
The sage is aware of the limits imposed upon our condition, of the
fact that we cannot describe the indescribable, and also understands
that the opposites are such only insofar as their modality is
concerned. At a more abstract level, they are the same.

The key phrase here is "at a more abstract level". When we claim that
the opposites are indistinguishable from each other, we always refer
to a given scope of application. At another scope of application, at
the level where their modality is made manifest and is pertinent, we
must treat them as distinct from each other. For example, in
interpersonal affairs we express feelings for others. When we say,
"you are my friend", the "you" refers to an abstraction which omits
the specifics of the given human organism (and all the specifics
within the specifics at increasingly more micro levels). This is why I
call it the "scope of application", because it determines what our
concepts apply to.

The sage, then, does not act to either create opposites or resolve
them. Such is a matter of the scope of application. It is not action
that creates those states of affairs, but the way of thinking.

Fundamentally, the sage "preaches the doctrine without words" because
there is nothing to describe. When we use words, when we name things,
including Being, we do it to communicate between ourselves. We are not
sages. With philosophy, we want to be friends of wisdom: that is
literally what "philosophy" means as philos (φίλος = friend) and
sophia (σοφία = wisdom). And with wisdom we understand the doctrine
which, somewhat anti-climactically, does not involve words. Our
humanity bounds us to the domain of Tropos, where we necessarily have
to use names and rely on language. We have to do this even for Being,
which we describe paradoxically as indescribable. There is a terminus
we cannot overcome. It is why the sage will not act to do something
about it. Nothing can be done about the terminus.

The sage knows the metaphysical truth that no statement, no matter how
clever, can ever express in full. I write "knows" because there is no
other possibility that is conceivable: all is, and all that is has
"is" in common.

As such, the sage is not triggered into action based on stimuli. The
sage is tranquil, in a state of ataraxia, which literally means
"non-disturbance" in Greek. Non-disturbance here has to do with the
creation or resolution of opposites and the essence of the cosmos. The
sage knows that everything that has Tropos is subject to
transfiguration: it comes and goes. The sage, therefore, cannot become
upset about the loss of something or, conversely, be elated by the
gain of something else. We do not own anything, not even our self. I
offered the latest explanation of this concept here:
<https://protesilaos.com/interpretations/2022-07-12-lhasa-bells/>.

## On not holding onto anything

We continue reading from this website:
<https://www.wendangwang.com/doc/e9374217c57a304ef32b37fc1fa3883371076c9e/2>.

    [ORIGINAL]
    Accomplishes, but claims no credit.
    It is because he lays claim to no credit
    That the credit cannot be taken away from him.

This passage is a continuation of my final remarks on the sage's
disposition from the previous section. Basically, the sage takes no
credit because, strictly speaking, there is nothing to take, nothing
to give, nothing to appropriate. It is not ours.

However, what I like about this passage is the human quality of the
sage. Every moron has a huge ego and yearns for the praise of others.
The sage, however, knows that there is nothing to be gained from the
opinions of others, be they favourable or not. Opinions make no
difference on the fact that we own nothing. Opinions cannot change
Being and Tropos.

The poetic way that ancient Greeks would present this theme is by
appealing to a god to tell them what to say. For example, Homer
(author of Iliad and Odyssey), asks from the Muse (Muses are the
goddesses of art, letters, and science; "muse" is found in "music" and
"museum", by the way) to sing for him. It is not that Homer or others
hallucinated about some woman in the sky reciting poetry. It was a way
to take away the egoism from the human: the person is just a
messenger, a combiner and amplifier of existing data, who can take no
real credit for the message being conveyed. The sage then, could be
repeating the words of Athena, the goddess of wisdom, or Apollon, the
god of harmony, and so on.

The specifics of the poetic statement do not matter. The idea is to
not exalt the sage as a god. No. We must focus on wisdom as such, not
the person who is wise. The person can help us befriend wisdom (be
philosophers), but we ultimately want wisdom, not the sage. Hence,
no-one takes credit. In a manner of speaking, nothing can be taken
away from the sage because the sage, like everybody else, owns
nothing. To continue with the poetic metaphor, it is the gods who
decide such matters.

    [ORIGINAL]
    Exalt not the wise,
    So that the people shall not scheme and contend;
    Prize not rare objects,
    So that the people shall not steal;
    Shut out from site the things of desire,
    So that the people's hearts shall not be disturbed.
    
    Therefore in the government of the Sage:
    He keeps empty their hearts
    Makes full their bellies,
    Discourages their ambitions,
    Strengthens their frames;
    So that the people may be innocent of knowledge and desires.
    And the cunning ones shall not presume to interfere.
    By action without deeds
    May all live in peace.

Here we have the ethical, else political, continuation of what we
covered before. Through wisdom, we learn that nothing is ours. We do
not exalt the wise, because we know that wisdom is not theirs; we do
not prize material possessions as obsessing over them makes us forget
the lesson we learnt about non-ownership. In practice, these lessons
can help those who are not sages approximate the state of ataraxia.
They will not be tranquil in the way a sage is, but they can at least
come to better terms with their existence.

I should note here that ethics and politics are indistinguishable in
practice. We analyse them as two distinct fields, in the same way we
do when we employ terms such as "the society" which is distinct in our
mind from "the economy". But when we consider things in vivo we cannot
have politics without moral claims. We also cannot have standalone
morality, as it pertains to affairs with an intersubjective scope.
There is no standalone subject and there can be no private ethics, in
the strict sense. The type of ethics which assumes a certain
relationship between a person and some deity is still intersubjective:
the person has no standalone presence.

    [ORIGINAL]
    Tao is a hollow vessel,
    And its use is inexhaustible!
    Fathomless!
    Like the fountain head of all things,
    Its sharp edges rounded off,
    Its tangles untied,
    Its light tempered,
    Its turmoil submerged,
    Yet dark like deep water it seems to remain.
    I do not know whose Son it is,
    An image of what existed before God.

This section describes what I have already explained about everything
partaking of Being. Even if we learn about all things, Being will
remain indescribable, "dark like deep water". Otherwise, we identify
Being indirectly in everything that has Tropos.

The final line in this passage is the same idea as what I already
wrote about god or gods in the previous section: deities are presences
with modality and, ipso facto, are not absolute.

    [ORIGINAL]
    Nature is unkind:
    It treats the creation like sacrificial straw-dogs.
    The Sage is unkind:
    He treats the people like sacrificial straw-dogs.
    How the universe is like a bellows!
    Empty, yet it gives a supply that never fails;

I think we should not take "unkind" to mean evil, vengeful, and the
like. It is a metaphorical, albeit blunt, way of saying that
everything around us, including ourselves, transfigures regardless of
how we feel about it. For me and you there is life and death, given
our scope of application. In terms of the cosmos as a whole, there is
no life and death: the cosmos always is. Presences constantly come and
go. Though what is, is---and that cannot change.

This is why the universe is fundamentally "empty", because its essence
is Being, which is indescribable and lacking in Tropos. Notice the use
of the term "fundamentally" and remember the scope of application. If
we are talking about specific things, the universe is full. Again,
this is a way of thinking, which is what the sage embodies as
practice.

Presences in the universe are in a state of constant transfiguration,
hence the supply that never fails. I should clarify though that this
supply does not come from nothing, does not occur in nothing, and does
not go towards or into nothing. If something is in any way related to
nothing, there would then be an abstraction of nothingness, which
would have to exist, and fundamentally all that exists is of Being.
Therefore, if we say that something is coming from nothing, either we
are wrong, or we use inaccurate language to describe how something
partakes of Being. We can, however, as a figure of speech say that "it
came out of nothing/nowhere" to mean that specifically it was not
there before, though something else always was.

This constant supply does not increase the stock of presences, so to
speak. Transfiguration can be described in plain terms as recycling.
In modern Greek we use an expression which translates us "everything
around us changes and all stay the same" (όλα γύρω αλλάζουνε κι όλα
ίδια μένουν). For the cosmos as whole, nothing changes. What is, is.
Again, we have to consider the scope of application. For specific
levels of abstraction, change happens continuously.

Without understanding the scope of application, all this would appear
nonsensical.

## On matters of selfhood and multifacetedness

Reading from
<https://www.wendangwang.com/doc/e9374217c57a304ef32b37fc1fa3883371076c9e/3>.

    [ORIGINAL]
    The more it is worked, the more it brings forth.
    By many words is wit exhausted.
    Rather, therefore, hold to the core.

I believe the first sentence alludes to the intellect, though I would
generalise its insight for everything that involves a degree of
sophistication or expertise. Regardless of talent or natural ability,
we get better at a given task by continuously practising it. Though
hard work must be smart as well. There has to be a proper method
involved.

The second and third sentences describe me well as an intellectual who
is keen on action. I skip endless writings that do not get to the
point---you know, when you start reading a book, are in page 50 and
still do not understand what the subject matter is... There is an
expression for this in English: "beating around the bush". It does not
work for me. I also dislike language that is designed to impress
and/or confuse. Profundity is not a function of how fancy our words
are, but whether we communicate clearly and accurately, with the
understanding that "clearly and accurately" are subject to
practicality.

At any rate, I consider this a preference, not a general truth.
Perhaps then, tiring out one's wits with many words that do not really
say anything is not a problem unto itself but only with respect to its
consequences. When the intellect is distracted by endless chatter they
do not have the capacity to grasp abstractions and everything I
already covered. This has knock-on effects on ethics/politics as those
who do not proceed from wisdom will not behave properly, leading to
undesirable states of affairs.

    [ORIGINAL]
    The Spirit of the Valley never dies.
    It is called the Mystic Female.
    The Door of the Mystic Female
    Is the root of Heaven and Earth.
    Continuously, continuously,
    It seems to remain.
    Draw upon it
    And it serves you with ease.

I cannot understand this part. Maybe the "Female" here is Yin in its
many meanings?

Personally, I am not fond of anthropomorphic (human-looking) metaphors
as they distract us from what we should be focusing on. Especially
when they invite us to think of a person's physical attributes, man,
woman, good looking, and so on. For example, Athena, the goddess of
wisdom, is said to be ever-virgin. Sure, there is a metaphor there
though its literal interpretation can easily distract us from noticing
it. As such, it is a bad metaphor. If we try to interpret said
virginity too much, we simply make the mistake of associating cultural
norms with abstractions: we humanise the abstraction to the point
where we forget it is not human---it is an abstraction.

To describe abstractions in human terms is understandable because it
has didactic and mnemonic value. The key is to never lose sight of the
metaphor. We want to employ figurative speech only to the extent that
it facilitates our work to communicate the true meaning of the subject
matter. For instance, I mentioned before how the Muse is the one who
tells Homer what to write: it is okay to describe the muses as women,
because it is easier to understand them that way, but the real lesson
in Homer's words is about non-ownership, as I pointed out before, not
women whispering in your ear.

    [ORIGINAL]
    The universe is everlasting.
    The reason the universe is everlasting
    Is that it does not life for Self.
    Therefore it can long endure.

This passage is more clear. The universe is everlasting as there is no
other possibility. It is impossible for something to come from
nothing, because nothing would then be something, with the potential
to beget something else, i.e. cause and effect. An absolute nothing
that is would then be the same as Being, but with a different name
(with the proviso that Being is indescribable and the name we use is
conventional). By "the same", I mean it would be the abstraction that
is most abstract and is thus identified in all the particulars, as I
explained earlier.

    [ORIGINAL]
    Therefore the Sage puts himself last,
    And finds himself in the foremost place;
    Regards his body as accidental,
    And his body is thereby preserved.
    Is it not because he does not live for Self
    That his Self is realized?

This is another take on the topic of non-ownership. The sage
understands that the body is not theirs. As I have written before,
nothing is ours, not even our self. The sage knows this and thus
refrains from clinging on to the impression that the body is their
own.

The allusion to preservation here is either an issue with translation
or an inconsistency in the original text. I cannot see how the sage's
body is preservable, given cosmic transfiguration, and I cannot fathom
how the sage's knowledge of non-ownership has any impact on this
matter. As such, I register my disagreement with the author or the
translator: the preservation of a body in its particular instantiation
does not happen. It does not even happen second-by-second, if you
think of its constituents coming and going.

In technical terms, we have to consider what "preservation" would even
entail. Science tells us that the body is not singular. It is a system
of systems, each of which has different life cycles (e.g. cells live
and die at a different rate than we do). Further, the body hosts
benign microorganisms which also have a life of their own. If we were
to reproduce the body, would we be reproducing all those then in their
exact configurations? And which snapshot should we pick that is the
true representative sample of this body?

But wait, because it does not stop there. The body does not have a
standalone presence. It exists within an environment: it is influenced
and determined by the environment. Gravity, for example, governs the
length of the bones, how fluids flow and organs work, and the like. We
know it because astronauts suffer the consequences of a different
level of gravity than the Earth's. So to preserve or replicate the
body we need to also do the same for its environment. By extension, we
must continue with the environment of the environment until we
eventually have to reproduce the entire cosmos.

Again, I disagree with the notion of "preserve" if that was the
intended meaning. If something is lost in translation, I am happy to
revise this point.

The last two sentences where the question is raised are fecund. What
they tell is that the sage has a clear perspective: they do not pursue
wrong objectives, such as fame and flattery. As such, the sage is
honest which allows their self to be realised: the signal in the
noise, if you will.

Though I personally hold that there is no true self. The self always
is a subjective narrative and subjectivity necessarily is
intersubjectvity due to the fact that human does not---and
cannot---have a standalone presence. While I agree that honesty is the
right perspective, I do not think that a true self will ever be
revealed as a constant, regardless of how sincere one is. Selfhood is
circumstantial. It is informed, framed, influenced, conditioned, or
otherwise determined by factors that are not limited to it. Whatever
its state, it is specific to the constitution of the case. And,
remember the notion of "snapshots": if it is changing, then the true
self would necessarily only be the common in the multitude of all
snapshots, which is no longer a particular snapshot in the same way
that "dog" is an abstraction without too many details so it applies to
all specimens but "this specific dog" is a given specimen.

One can be "true" in the sense of not being pretentious, not following
the fashions just to fit in, not providing assent to false views
simply because they find currency in their milieu, and so on. Still,
this kind of truth is another way of expressing honesty; honesty to
the best of our abilities in the given situation, which still happens
within the overarching constraints of being affected by factors
outside our control. Honesty is a disposition, not a perfect state. We
want to be honest, but we cannot be non-human through honesty;
non-human in the sense of transcending the human condition of
messiness.

If anything, the sage would be aware of this relativity and would come
to the conclusion that one must not take themselves too seriously. By
that I mean that the sage will acknowledge their humanity: the fact
that its nature is influenced and determined by factors external to
it; the fact that the self is not truly in control of all that is
happening to it; the fact that the self is not immutable; the fact
that the sage is all too human.

Even the wisest of the wise will not be wisdom incarnate, because
wisdom is an abstraction while that which is incarnate has a Tropos
that is not limited to wisdom. Remember what I wrote about the
multifacetedness of the human condition: intellect, passions, body,
spirit, etc. Human is a bit of everything, without exceptions.

Wisdom, then, consists in finding a practical (i.e. imperfect and
case-specific) balance between all the facets of the human condition.
One cannot be singularly intellectual. One must show emotion when the
situation calls for it and one must treat the body with care. This is
not mere instruction. We know from everyday experience how, for
example, a tired body makes it impossible to concentrate and thus
think about something profound. We know that a disturbed emotional
state either is caused by or causes effects on the body which lead to
a vicious cycle, again affecting how we think of things. Put simply,
health is a balance between all the facets; a balance that is
sustainable over the long-term ("long-term" in human years, meaning
that we do not change from one week to the next or something along
those lines). And health is catalytic in how selfhood is conceived.
Again, the self is not "true" in some decontextualised sense. By
"decontextualised" I mean standalone, as in not accounting for the
factors of the case that contribute to its presence.

    [ORIGINAL]
    The best of men is like water;
    Water benefits all things
    And does not compete with them.
    It dwells in (the lowly) places that all disdain -
    Wherein it comes near to the Tao.
    In his dwelling, (the Sage) loves the (lowly) earth;
    In his heart, he loves what is profound;
    In his relations with others, he loves kindness;
    In his words, he loves sincerity;
    In government, he loves peace;
    In business affairs, he loves ability;
    In his actions, he loves choosing the right time.
    It is because he does not contend
    That he is without reproach.

I think this passage is clear and does not require any further
commentary, given what I have already expounded on.

Continuing with what I was explaining above, the sage remains a human.
We use "the sage" in these notes a lot, though we do not mean to exalt
it to some divine status. It is why I prefer the term "philosopher",
which means friend of wisdom. It speaks of an attitude rather than an
attribute and in so doing it recognises the imperfection of the human
condition. I am a philosopher because I want to live in accordance
with wisdom, though because of my humanity I may have a mistaken view
of what wisdom is, I may make the wrong choices, and so on.

The sage. then, is an ideal for us to communicate certain principles.
We should not treat wise people as infallible---they are not. Nor
should we trivialise their wisdom, dimissing them as "you are just
human like me", because that leaves out too many important details.

## Avoiding exaggerations

Reading from:
<https://www.wendangwang.com/doc/e9374217c57a304ef32b37fc1fa3883371076c9e/4>.

    [ORIGINAL]
    Stretch (a bow) to the very full,
    And you will wish you had stopped in time.
    Temper a (sword-edge) to its very sharpest,
    And the edge will not last long.

What we learn from this passage is to conduct ourselves in moderation.
When something goes to an extreme, it no longer has a positive effect
on us. The way I describe this principle is by remarking that the
difference between remedy and poison is one of degree. A pill will
address the headache. A bottle of pills will kill you.

Everything must be done in moderation, including philosophy. Remember,
once again, the multifaceted nature of humanity. If we spend all day
philosophising, we will not have enough time to care for the body, or
we might miss emotionally fulfilling experiences. That would
ultimately be to our detriment. If we ignore the body, it will become
unhealthy, and that will undermine our ability to philosophise. If we
neglect emotions, we will turn too rationalistic and lose the
sensitivity we have for what others can bring to our life. Without
emotion, we also forgo the nuance in experiences such as the
observation of the sunset or the admiration of a full moon. That sense
of joy and awe belongs to our emotional facet. Besides, is it not
exciting to study? Excitement is an emotion.

We then want to find the virtuous middle ground in our life. This is
not an exact measurement that is true for all people. It is a
disposition, a way of living. Our intention is to avoid exaggerations
and learn to trust in our own devices, while keeping an open mind for
what others may have to say. If we are working all day, our body tells
us to stop. Perhaps we should work half as much. If we are getting too
excited, maybe we are developing an addiction for that exhilarating
experience. Can we then detach ourselves from it? Are we really in
control past that moment? You get the idea.

In the Greek tradition the concept of a moderate life is developed in
large part through artistic representation. A human who would go to
the extremes is said to commit hubris (ύβρις). "Hubris" describes the
peculiar brand of cockiness that leads us to defy the limits imposed
on us by our nature.

Hubris is often framed in religious terms because those are easier to
understand and remember, compared to the austere Delphic maxim
"Nothing in excess" (μηδέν άγαν, which would more literally be
"nothing led" or, more loosely, "nothing [to be led away] in deviation
of the mean"). The gods place limits on humans and punish them if they
go beyond them. The gist here is not that gods are micromanaging our
lives and are actively trying to harm us. This is the poet's way of
saying that our nature limits us. We are not omnipotent.

We can reason about hubris without reference to gods. Belief in the
gods is neither necessary nor relevant. For example, someone who
drives a motorcycle at full speed on a wet road without wearing a
helmet is committing hubris. If the vehicle loses traction, the driver
is most probably dead. We can say that this person is going to anger
Zeus, for example, but this is a mental shortcut to describing the
wider phenomenon.

Hubris is a practical idea which encapsulates the insight that
moderation is a central theme in our life.

In matters of politics and life in general, I am a pragmatist. Not
because I do not like the idea of paradise on earth, so to speak, but
due to the fact that the human condition makes us imperfect. For as
long as we have this modality, we must deal with it. There will be
conflicts, there will be erroneous judgements. Not because we are

to our conduct, which is influenced by our way of thinking.

Furthermore, our nature---and Tropos in general---yields diversity. We
cannot expect everyone to be a sage. Just as we cannot turn every
person into a painter, a nurse, an engineer, an elite athlete, and so
on. Each individual has a unique set of skills. By letting them
cultivate those skills we are better off collectively. Why? Because we
can then learn more about the human condition. If everyone I meet is
like me, I am not discovering something new: I develop tunnel vision,
which makes me less wise by obscuring from my view the diversity of
the world around me. But if one is a pianist, another a fashionista, a
third an accountant, etc. I gain insight into perspectives and modes
of expression I would otherwise be oblivious to. This makes me more
aware of what is around me and conditions me to think that there may
be more. Again, we are not omniscient and our diversity helps us
broaden our horizons. We do it together, as societies, as cultures,
and as a species.

This extends the critique I have for the exaltation of the sage and
why I prefer the disposition of philosophy (as "friend of wisdom").
For instance, I want the musician to give me what I cannot produce
myself---music---and ask from them to do it as best they can. One can
learn the basics of philosophy, such as the practical comments I make,
and be a better version of themselves in what they do.

phenomena, which can be summarised in the image of a deity, like
Demetra for the Earth Mother we always depend on but instead of saying
"I need food, water, a healthy environment, etc." I just say "I have
Demetra by my side" or something along those lines. The many
gods/archetypes, just like the many talents of humans and the
multitude of phenomena, are not necessarily mutually exclusive. They
all partake of pattern.

A final point on "less is more" from my experience with designing
colour schemes, themes, and a typeface. In any given context, if
everything is emphasised nothing will stand out: the emphasis of one
cancels out the emphasis of the other. In such a scenario, the most
emphatic is that which has no emphasis, because it stands out through
the absence of that attribute. Similarly, to emphasise the emphasis is
to exaggerate, to burden the user with needless extras. We can think
of those as having more of something, even though it is not needed.
And, as with the difference between remedy and poison, having too much
of something is deleterious.

## Austerity and distractions

Continuing from:
<https://www.wendangwang.com/doc/e9374217c57a304ef32b37fc1fa3883371076c9e/5>.

    [ORIGINAL]
    The five colors blind the eyes of man;
    The five musical notes deafen the ears of man;
    The five flavors dull the taste of man;
    Horse-racing, hunting and chasing madden the minds of man;
    Rare, valuable goods keep their owners awake at night.
    Therefore the Sage:
    Provides for the belly and not the eye.
    Hence, he rejects the one and accepts the other.

I think this section is a continuation of what I covered in the
previous section about doing things in moderation. The wise approach
is to get enough to sustain ourselves, but not more that we grow
addicted to it.

All the extreme experiences evoke strong reactions, which affect our
behaviour. Consider a favourite dish. If it is too delicious, we
cannot stop thinking about it. We yearn for the moment when it is time
to eat it. It keeps us busy and thus detracts from other matters. Same
principle for everything we become so deeply invested in that it takes
over our whole life.

While food may be used figuratively in the original text, I personally
do have an explicitly austere approach on the matter (and "austerity"
is an accurate descriptor of my life and what I represent). It comes
down to simplicity. What we eat must be bland. Not too oily, not too
salty, not too spicy, not too sweet, etc. Basic ingredients and
generic techniques (e.g. only boil vegetables if necessary). Boring
and uneventful food keeps us healthy without distracting us from other
activities. Why? Because it is boring and we do not want to think
about it any further. Whereas delicious food keeps calling for our
attention.

Same principle for music. If we listen to loud songs all day, silence
will disturb us. Meditation will be troublesome. Quietude will feel
eerie. There is a time for art and a time for philosophy; a time for
fun and a time for contemplation. We must slow things down and let the
moment have our undivided attention.

In short, all thrilling experiences have to be kept in check as they are
the most likely to lead us astray, far away from a moderate lifestyle.

lack perspective on what the needs of the people are.

Sure! Good luck finding a single one to do the job!

As with all matters, politics needs to be done in moderation. Not

some order, starting with a collective understanding of their basic
moral code). Do not let the ideal give you a false sense of security

## The role of the sage

Continuing from:
<https://www.wendangwang.com/doc/e9374217c57a304ef32b37fc1fa3883371076c9e/6>.

    [ORIGINAL]
    Nor by its sinking, is there darkness.

This passage is another comment on the Tao/Being. Paradoxes, such as

As humans, our terminus is Tropos. We cannot escape it. Even in
speaking of Being, we give it a name---"Being"---yet maintain that it
is nameless. We assign an attribute to it, else a description, by
holding that Being is "that which is most abstract" and "that which is
not describable". In other words, we give it Tropos while saying that

The alternative is to remain silent. Though even then we grasp it in
our mind through Tropos.

previously, Being, and everything predicated it on it as Tropos, is
the only possibility. There can be no nothingness, strictly speaking.

While this part starts by alluding to the sages of old, I believe the
real meaning is the paradoxical indescribable nature of the most
profound. This continues the theme of Tao/Being. For humans though,
who as humans necessarily have Tropos, it is always possible to
describe and render intelligible that which they say.

The sage may be likened to an eagle: a bird that flies higher than all
other birds. Yet the eagle can descend to the lower altitudes to meet
the others. Because the sage is aware of non-ownership, as I explained
in the previous sections, they have no reason to think that those
heights are their exclusivity. The sage can help others to at least
imagine what it is like to be up there.

Here allow me to introduce the concept of the "Promethean Ideal" that
I have inferred based on the myth of Prometheus. This is a titan,
basically a god, who brought knowhow to humanity. The "knowhow" is
encapsulated in the myth as the art of using fire. Equipped with their
newfound knowledge, humans could do more things than was ever possible
before. Thanks to Prometheus' gift, they could take the next step in
becoming more sophisticated. The ideal then is for us to learn from
Prometheus so that we always try to share knowhow and knowledge in
general. We must try to help humanity become better: to be more
god-like, so to speak. Though we know that people are imperfect and
all knowledge available to them can be used malevolently. Prometheus
understands this but still foresees a world where humanity moves from
a primitive to a more advanced understanding of itself and the world
at-large.

Why would we do that? Why not take advantage of knowhow to pursue our
own ends? Because a god, who is a higher being, has shown us that
through sharing we collectively improve our condition; a god has shown
us not to worry about ownership: the knowhow of fire is not mine or
yours, but is available to everybody.

I think the sage and generally the person who has a disposition of
altruism is this Promethean figure in practice. They can give the rest
of us what we lack and they can show us how to make best use of it by
embodying it themselves. Eventually, we will learn and move closer to
wisdom. And, as with Prometheus, this involves personal sacrifice
which is about letting go of the precious self we think we can hold
onto forever.

    [ORIGINAL]
    Who can find repose in a muddy world?
    By lying still, it becomes clear.
    Who can maintain his calm for long?
    By activity, it comes back to life.
    He who embraces this Tao
    Guards against being over-full.
    Because he guards against being over-full,
    He is beyond wearing out and renewal.
    Attain the utmost in Passivity,
    Hold firm to the basis of Quietude.
    The myriad things take shape and rise to activity,

    But returns to the root (soil) from which it springs.
    To return to the root is Repose;

To me this section starts with the recognition that the domain of
Tropos is one of incessant change: transfiguration on a cosmic scale.
Something comes, something goes, something deforms, something else
forms. There is no creation from nothing or in nothing, and there is
no transition to nothing. What is, is. Ever-present, ever-lasting. It
is the mode of being that changes.

In this sense, the common in the multitude of this state of flux is
motion or reform or transition. The very pattern of change is constant
in itself: it remains change and never becomes non-change. By
continuing with the pattern seeking, we identify such constants, which
form an abstract structure with which we make sense of the cosmos.

The cosmos has "logos", which is the Greek word for "rate", "ratio",
"reason" (as reasonable and as a cause), and "speech", else
"language". These concepts all describe the domain of Tropos: whatever
happens has Logos built into it. It is immanent. Logos, then, is this
"pattern as such" that I alluded to earlier, an alias for Being.

You may think that rate, ratio, causality are obvious. But what about
language? Language is immanent as well. In its most basic form,
language describes a feedback loop: something happens and something
else occurs in response to it. It is binary. The concatenation of such
bits, per computer science parlance, is what contributes to the
interplay of factors in any given constitution of the case. We can
think of a case as a network of communication channels which yields
specific meanings, else forms and modality. It is how a system can
have phenomena that are specific to its scope of application and not
to the more particular domain of each of its factors in isolation: the
given bits engender different meanings through their exact
combinations or sequences.

Logos is constant then. We then realise that there is no difference
between the two extremes of the immovable world that Parmenides talked
about and the ever-changing one that Heraclitus described (based on
the surviving fragments we have, anyway). The two sages were
elaborating on different facets of a singular reality. They were
simply operating at different scopes of application, which I am
confident they understood all too well.

Couched in those terms, what the text above is telling us is that the
sage can discern both (i) the transfigurable nature of things, such as
by admitting to non-ownership, and the (ii) universal magnitudes.

For our everyday life, the insight is that we will stress out and
become ineffective if we are afraid of losing what we have. We do not
really have anything that is ours in an inalienable way. Circumstances
can give us wealth and take it away from us. We may be healthy or
sickly. Our conception of self depends on how our body works and what
our environment is (points I have already explained in the previous
sections). We escape from the fear of losing things when we admit that
transfiguration is ceaseless. We then conduct ourselves in moderation,
both for the so-called "good times" and the "bad" ones. It it thus
possible to be tranquil, undisturbed by the fluctuations in the world,
because we have seen beyond the transfigurable into the abstract
structure---the constants---of the cosmos: Logos. Hence, with wisdom
we are, in the words of the translated text, "beyond wearing out and
renewal".

This is a direct continuation of what I explained about finding the

incessant change as we discern the abstract structure in the world. We

the cosmic transfiguration. Tropos is a constant, Logos is constant.

because we do not cling on to anything and, therefore, have no worry

nature. I agree with the salient point, though we must be careful with
our biases. Sometimes we will have a dogmatic view of what is and what
is not natural. We will be using "nature" as a pretext for---and
justification of---our own prejudices. The key then, is to remain
honest about our disposition. When we find that we are strongly
attached to something, we need to remain mindful of non-ownership.
When we fiercely disagree with another person, we ought to mutually
take a step back in acknowledging the fact we are not omniscient. And
so on. We do make mistakes and are inherently fallible. What saves us
from ruin is the commitment to a balanced lifestyle. We avoid the
extremes, we guard ourselves against hubris, and we proceed with a
spirit of sincerity and compassion.

terms, we move away from nature and we lose sense of the basic things

Perhaps it is counter-productive to argue against notions such as
humanity and justice, because as humans we cannot be apolitical. For
as long as we have to manage interpersonal affairs, we will have
politics; politics in the basic sense of a public sphere, else the
presence of---and concern for---the commons.

By "apolitical", I do not mean basic activities like not voting for a
party or not reading the news. Those we can afford. But even when we
do not participate in the political process, we live in an instituted
reality. Our life is framed by legal-cultural arrangements. There are
rules which define roles and roles delimit the set of expectations on
what is and is not applicable or acceptable behaviour in the given
context. There are many people who do not care about politics, yet
their intersubjective experience is underpinned by politics. They are
not apolitical beings operating in an apolitical context, strictly
speaking.

Maybe then, we need a more practical example to realise how far we
have deviated from nature. Consider how modern culture basically
everywhere values material possessions to an extreme. Contrary to the
insight of non-ownership, we have a dogma of ownership. We judge other
people on the basis of how much they appear to have. The more, the
better. Such is the principle. It is a value system where the actual
person does not matter as a human being with talents and
sensitivities. What counts is the appearance of affluence, the
pretence of ownership. I write "pretence" because even the richest
person owns nothing. Everything is alienable. So it is a pretence, a
lie, a delusion, a shadow play at the social level.

People who only care about conforming with this hypocrisy have the
wrong perspective. In other humans they only see facilitators or
antagonists on the path to showing off. With so-called "social media",
even private affairs are commodified as a medium of exchange that
grants one short-term validation. Validation of this sort is reduced
to the shared belief that they are owning "stuff". This "stuff" can be
a car, a house, a fancy job title, the positive opinion of others, et
cetera.

This society values everything that is hard and fast, big and loud. It
all needs to be cranked up to the maximum. There is no nuance, no
subtlety. Think about paintings, for example. I hear people remark on
a naturalist landscape how it does not have "intense colours". There
is nothing wrong with more saturation per se. But if one is
insensitive to more subtle hues, we already sense how they cannot
appreciate the finer experiences in life: a sunset by the beach, birds
chirping on the mountains, a walk in the forest...

When all we see in other people is a means to an end, we cannot
possibly have a better view of non-human life forms. We treat them as
lesser than us. Humanity views the planet as an expendable resource
and is destroying it in the process. There are those who understand,
but the vast majority are not willing to change their lifestyle even
while preaching about its merits.

The point is that we lack perspective. We have a set of values that
glorifies ownership: we thus do not recognise the cosmic truth of
non-ownership. We believe we are better than other forms of life: we
do not admit that we are just as transfigurable, just as
interdependent and coexistent. We want everything to be extreme: we
lose sight of the virtuous middle ground and commit hubris in the
process. Our civilisation is an intricate superstructure built on top
of misunderstandings about how things stand in the world.

This is the conclusion which continues from the aforementioned. Again,
I would not dwell on the concepts of "humanity" and "justice". The
gist is that we must remain open to the truth. Lies may give us
immediate profit, but all of it is an illusion in the end.

The mode of truth-seeking leads us to the rejection of modern values.
We will no longer seek validation through possessions. One does not
need to be validated for being. We will stop obsessing about ownership
and not judge ourselves on the basis of such a metric. Instead, we
will remain tranquil and be freed from the burden of clinging on to
stuff that can never be truly owned. In short, we will have a more
balanced disposition which will prepare us to lead simpler and more
peaceful lives.

As I noted before, not everyone will become a sage. Humans are diverse
and each individual has a unique set of talents. An ideal state where
we all live in ataraxia will not happen. We should at least aspire to
our highest. Try to be more god-like in our disposition, more
compassionate and understanding, more patient, more tolerant, and less
egoistic. We will seek to be friends of wisdom, even though we
understand it is not possible to ever become wisdom incarnate. In
doing so, we will avoid distractions.
