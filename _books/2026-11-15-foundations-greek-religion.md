Religiosity is a collective affair. Through religion, each person
comes into contact with fellow human beings within a framework of
shared beliefs. The values that govern the community constitute the
common good which all members recognise and strive towards. We have
the prerequisites of community when we share common values. We then
find each another through rituals so that the community is embedded
both as locality and cultural specificity over the long term, and thus
as ethnicity.

Values must be grounded in human nature so that every human being
across the earth and regardless of era may embrace them. Consequently,
values are derived as a diagnosis of the individual&rsquo;s reality, of
interpersonal relationships, of social trends, and of life in general.
A religion that does not accept the natural state creates
contradictions in human consciousness. Those lead to confusion and
impasses that bring about enfeeblement of the person and, ultimately,
resignation from the struggle of everyday life.

The foundations of religion must be comprehensible, at least to some
degree, by everyone. Only in this way can we resist ideas, programmes,
or stratagems that lead to cultural decline and, by extension,
biological extinction. We remain anchored in the essence of our faith
so that we maintain the necessary clarity and rigour. We conduct
rituals and celebrate at regular intervals to keep the community
active. We stay committed to our values and do not seek the easy way
out in every new spiritual trend or imported fad that promises much
and delivers nothing.

The Greek religion is the faith of responsibility. A person who does
not acknowledge their responsibilities cannot be called an ethnic
Greek (Έλληνας εθνικός). Here begins our strictness and our zero
tolerance. Consequently, we are not asking, we are telling.

I lead by example, applying these ideas to my daily life. As a
philosopher, I assume the responsibility of utilising the talents I
have been endowed with by nature. My purpose is to clarify thoughts,
establish the framework, and provide guiding lines. I do so for the
people of today as well as for all those to come. I dare to voice an
opinion, in full awareness of the consequences.

Every human being must assume their responsibilities. Let the painter
show us how through painting we can depict truth, let the physician
promote health already through their lifestyle as well as their
profession, let the priest organise the worshippers guided by the
common good rather than personal gain, let the woman contribute
tirelessly to social reproduction, that is, biological and cultural
perpetuation, and so on.

Our religiosity in the modern era aims to rekindle that flame which
created our civilisation from antiquity and kept it alive across
millennia. It is the ethos of frugality and the spirit of struggle. We
rediscover the essence of the three central Delphic maxims, which we
update for the needs of today:

-   **Nothing in excess (Μηδέν άγαν):** Avoid exaggerations.
-   **Know yourself (Γνώθι σεαυτόν):** Know who you are and where you stand.
-   **Certitude stands beside Ate (goddess of ruin) (Εγγύα παρά δ&rsquo;Άτα):** Complacency brings catastrophe.

What follows explains the above or is an extension of them.

# The sin of exaggeration

Human has a tendency to exaggerate. From this arises evil. We say that
&laquo;an apple a day keeps the doctor away&raquo;, yet we recognise that a
hundred apples a day would no longer be benign: excessive fruit
consumption leads to poisoning and death. Hence we know that the apple
is not an unconditional good. Its usefulness lies in the degree of its
consumption. That is, when we eat one apple we proceed with
moderation, within the natural limits of human nature, and thus we do
something good for ourselves. When we stray beyond the limits, we are
led to evil.

We believe in good and evil as magnitudes that are made manifest
through relationships (see [The exclusivity of love](#h:d37d7dd6-c2cc-4c7c-9332-5fe1263219e6)). Something that
otherwise appears good will turn into evil through exaggeration. Thus
we can understand the Greek word for &laquo;sin&raquo; (αμαρτία), which literally
means to miss the mark. We see that exaggeration (υπερβολή), which is
to shoot over the target, and &laquo;missing the mark&raquo; essentially describe
the same phenomenon.

By examining exaggeration, we can diagnose all the sins into which
human falls. Greed is the basic human impulse for possessiveness, to
acquire and use things, which is nonetheless taken to an extreme.
Sloth/laziness is human&rsquo;s daily need for pause and rest, which is
again indulged to an excessive degree. Murder is the expression of
hatred, lust, jealousy, or anyhow some other desire carried to excess.
Substance abuse is human&rsquo;s direct response to stimuli that ultimately
is led to exaggeration.

In all cases, we see that exaggeration develops feedback loops in a
person, actions and reactions that become self-invigorating, which
acquire a behaviour of their own. In essence, a person loses control
of their self, either partially or entirely. We can imagine each sin
as a spirit with its own will, which has now taken over the body and
is compelling it to perform all sorts of indignities. Hence the common
phenomenon where someone recognises their errors, knows how to correct
them, yet finds that they cannot effect the change.

A person who constantly goes to extremes does not control themselves
and is therefore unreliable. If they exaggerate in one domain, they
will tend to exaggerate in other domains as well, becoming
increasingly dangerous both to themselves and to others. Such a person
is harmful to themselves and potentially harmful to everyone, because
the degree of their deeds goes beyond moderation.

Exaggeration also exists in everyday human decisions that otherwise
seem innocent. We cannot philosophise constantly: we must act, labour
for the common good, and function within whatever limits we find
ourselves in. Anything we overdo ceases to be appropriate, even if in
its simple expression it is fine.

Religion establishes the framework to prevent such extremities. We
must help individuals who have a propensity for exaggeration, offering
them both affection and strict boundaries, while always maintaining
our discipline. We must demand compliance with our values and, more
practically, with the social structures that guide individuals: the
extended family, the school, local self-governance, and the religious
hierarchy, among others.

A person who refuses to function within those parameters, a person who
seeks to satisfy their every want to the detriment of the common good,
shall be punished as the community sees fit, without exception
whatsoever (see [We reject miracles and idolatry](#h:5b5e051d-eb86-4898-adf0-9fc384511a8d)).

# Human&rsquo;s relationship with the limits

Human does not define the limits. Those exist independently of our
desires and institutions. If, as already noted, eating too many apples
a day brings poisoning, then this is a reality from which we cannot
escape.

Here we have our first encounter with divinity, regardless of our
initial beliefs: there exists a world outside ourselves whose rules
are not subject to our volition. It does not matter what we think or
what we want. Limits will always be there, whether we like it or not.

Simply put, the gods ordain and do not ask for our opinion. Human is
neither a contributing factor in the condition that created humankind
nor a partner in cosmic becoming. Human functions under laws superior
to their will and is always subject to the impact of their environment
upon their actions.

Every human action has consequences. Those cannot be avoided. If, for
instance, someone consumes five bottles of wine, they will face
immediate and long-term problems that they will inevitably have to
live with (see [The sin of exaggeration](#h:9df22656-3512-41dc-b9ce-e12d4fedc51c)).

Here we encounter the unavoidable reality: human can neither abolish
nor transcend trade-offs. One cannot act without consequences, be it
for oneself or for others. Preceding actions determine anteceding
choices. Everything is framed by the factors of the environment. There
is no absolute freedom, only the capacity for choice within the
boundaries that arise from any given constitution of the case
(see [The Fates of chance, choice, and the inevitable](#h:60cf69e5-76bb-4535-b742-215a16e2ff07)).

Our religion starts with the recognition of reality. We acknowledge
that, yes, there are limits beyond us. We understand them as
archetypes. We give them names and, in a metaphorical manner, depict
them as persons with specific human traits. We want this artistic
portrayal because it carries didactic and mnemonic value. We can
remember everything more easily and teach it more effectively to
people of all skill levels.

How we name and represent the gods is our own affair. It is a cultural
event with particular ethnic or even local features. Nevertheless, we
are not the ones who make the gods. The limits are always there,
whether we believe in them or not.

Consequently, religiosity begins with our submission to the gods,
which arises from the realisation that limits exist. &laquo;Submission&raquo;
means recognising who we are in relation to where we stand. We admit
that we are neither the origin nor the epicentre of the world, but
merely another part of the whole.

We know that we are not equal to the gods, nor are we partners in
creation. We live with the prevailing conditions and parameters given
to us by the gods, without comprehending in absolute depth the cause
or end of our existence and of being in general. For our quotidian
life, the only thing we must always remember is responsibility, as it
is what keeps us within moderation.

The person who submits to the gods cultivates the right mindset,
expressed as a responsible way of life. They accept that their actions
and the relations within their environment lead both to good and to
evil. They recognise that every action, individual and collective,
carries consequences with which they must live, and thus that today&rsquo;s
recklessness creates tomorrow&rsquo;s disasters.

Responsibility has a personal dimension, but it usually becomes a
collective matter. If, for instance, an individual sets fire to our
forest, then all of us will suffer the consequences of that
disgraceful act. For this very reason, religion must be governed by
strictness: not allowing individuals to do whatever they wish,
reminding each person that life exists beyond their self, and standing
ready to impose penalties on those who do not respect the common good.
In other words, religion is collectivity standing against every
extreme form of individualism.

Religion enables collective judgement. It provides worshippers with
the values and pertinent teachings that allow them to discern the
beneficial from the harmful. If someone tends toward exaggeration, we
already know where this behaviour leads, and we are therefore prepared
to intervene before the situation becomes extreme. Exercising
judgement means pointing out what is good and what is evil for all of
us.

Every person must be aware of what is happening around them. Together,
we hold each other accountable, preventing the individual from turning
inward to individualism and acting in an excessive way at the expense
of the common good. Whoever exhibits apathy is untrustworthy and
likely dangerous, because they refuse to understand their surroundings
and will consequently fail to identify limits in a timely manner. That
is precisely where evil will emerge.

# We reject miracles and idolatry

Every society must resist the degenerative tendencies that human
desire begets. Human seeks easy solutions and, through laziness,
drifts toward folly and self-destruction. A person who does not wish
to work hard for their life and does not labour for the common good
will always seek a way out in supposed magical results and miracles.

Wherever there is demand for miracle-workers, a corresponding supply
emerges. It is quite literally a marketplace. Human&rsquo;s desire for the
effortless and easy brings into being the opportunist who sells out
everything to gratify their appetites. Miracle-workers sway corrupt
crowds and grow rich accordingly. Corruption begins as an otherwise
trivial turn towards comfort, which gradually morphs into contemptuous
and antisocial behaviour.

These desires also engender idolatry. Human seeks miraculous objects,
magic stones, healing bones, superfoods, wisdom without thought, and
anything else along the lines of shirking. Furthermore, this desire
forms the basis of every narrative about chosen peoples, so-called
races of gods, divine right of kings, and the like.

If we feel cold, it is because we stand in the shade. We therefore
take responsibility to move towards the light in order to warm
ourselves. This effort requires effort and often comes with prolonged
struggle (see [The symbolism of the Olympic Games](#h:300dcc96-5b3d-4eb0-8c1b-3aed11fb3211)). The sun does not
alter its course for anyone&rsquo;s sake. We must seek the gods and, through
the aforementioned submission, align ourselves with them. Thus we draw
energy from the cosmos in a manner analogous to how we create vitamins
from the sun.

We reject idolatry from the root to its branches because we do not
believe in miracles. Every human being and every community lives
within the limits ordained by the gods and experiences whatever the
prevailing conditions render possible. We emphasise the importance of
responsibility and exercise judgement accordingly (see [Human&rsquo;s relationship with the limits](#h:80fe2adb-dd3d-48bd-abda-c5c373a5d594)).

All artefacts of our art, such as statues and icons, are products of
human creativity. These objects may even be mass-produced
industrially, perhaps in cooperation with other nations. They possess
nothing special in themselves, and it does not trouble us if they
appear inexpensive. We need them for human&rsquo;s simple, everyday
aesthetic needs, as well as to teach more effectively the relevant
symbolisms that encode our values.

Our altars and temples must be plain and austere. Wherever people
adorned their temples with gold, they were ruined. Whatever objects we
use, we want them to be humble as well. Luxuries invariably divert
attention away from divinity. Corrupt is the person who worships
wealth and glory, and who consequently commits the exaggeration of
fancying themselves more important than they truly are. Only the gods
are to be venerated, while everything else must perform its function
as tool or memento.

Furthermore, frugality promotes sociability because it enables all
people to participate in the commons. We reject every manifestation of
the pay-to-win mindset. We never close our doors to the poor, nor do
we offer special favours to the wealthy. We are all equal before the
gods, as we all operate within the same limits.

The gods make no distinctions, since no human being merits special
treatment in the cosmic becoming. God has no need to prove anything to
anyone. The laws of the universe are not to be suspended, for there is
no person entitled to such special treatment.

The priest cannot intervene to alter these realities. The very idea
that someone can buy off god through prayers and lucrative offerings,
or engage in transactions and bargaining with it, is degrading and
utterly perilous. The gods are stern. Those who believe that divinity
must manifest in a specific manner solely for their sake have a warped
view of their self and of the world around them.

# The exclusivity of love

Human cannot avoid trade-offs, a truth that holds even in the purest
intentions and emotions. Let us focus our attention on love in order
to understand the reality of our nature in general (see [Human&rsquo;s relationship with the limits](#h:80fe2adb-dd3d-48bd-abda-c5c373a5d594)).

A universal symbol of love is a mother holding her baby. The child
cannot live without protection; it lacks the ability to feed or defend
itself. It depends on its mother for survival. The mother essentially
transfers her energy to the child, making it strong through her own
strength.

The mother&rsquo;s relationship with the baby is exclusive. That is, it
excludes from the transfer of energy every other form of life that is
not related to the infant. The mother will avoid dangers, keep the
child away from predators and monsters, and thereby demarcate a safe
perimeter within which only select individuals are granted access
under specific conditions.

The mother cannot express love towards everything, even if she naively
wished to. If, for example, she allows snakes, spiders, rats, bats,
wolves, and perverts near the child, the child will be injured and/or
die. The mother will not only exclude these life forms, but will also
strive for a clean environment so that disease does not develop. By
extension, she will seek a stable society and an institutional
framework that protects individuals, as well as a nation that
preserves its integrity through robust borders at its exterior as well
as law and order throughout its territory.

From this exclusive relationship alone, we can comprehend our world in
general. If we are honest with ourselves, we will admit that every
safe environment human constructs is created through the potentiality
of violence and maintained accordingly by potential violence. This
does not mean that everything is violent in practice, but merely that
matters end up there if all other peaceful means fail, such as
education, spiritual cultivation, and the rule of law.

Quite simply, if we are to build a home, we must clear our space of
all elements of wild nature that can harm us. We will drive out snakes
and rodents. We will banish mosquitoes. We will uproot every plant
that carries poison or other dangers. We will secure the perimeter
against thieves and invaders. In other words, we will do what is
required to be able to have love. This love will always be a
relationship of exclusivity, and we must maintain the necessary
vigilance to be decisive in service of the common good.

Love towards all is the kind of stupidity that welcomes the wolf into
the flock and cheers in favour of disease and extinction. We do not
accept it though, above all, we do not tolerate any belief that
rationalises or beautifies irresponsibility.

# The Fates of chance, choice, and the inevitable

Human does not exercise complete control over their world. They are
subject to the laws of nature. Every experience is framed by the
prevailing conditions of the situation. Yet they are not entirely
helpless, for they retain a measure of initiative. Our ancestors
understood human&rsquo;s relationship with their world as the influence of
the three fates. They gave them names and portrayed them artistically
as three sisters: Lachesis (Λάχεσις), Clotho (Κλωθώ), and Atropos
(Άτροπος).

There exists in the world the element of chance, the tendency of
elements toward differentiation, expressed as entropy, as well as the
incessant transfiguration of every presence. This is what Lachesis
symbolises. The name itself relates to allotment, the lottery, or the
colloquial phrase &laquo;if it so happens&raquo; (άμα λάχει).

Chance governs our lives, but it does not operate alone. We possess
initiative, which leads to actions that entail consequences. Prior
actions frame and/or determine subsequent actions. If, for instance, I
choose now the path leading to the mountain or to the seaside, this
decision determines what I will see in the immediate future, between a
mountainscape and a seascape. This is what Clotho symbolises, whom we
can picture as weaving fabric where the initial pattern, like drawing
a straight line, already predetermines what we can drawn afterwards.
For example, a straight line in phase one precludes a circle in phase
two, or at least the future circle requires the undoing of the line
and the creating of a curve from the beginning.

Beyond choices and chance, there always exist those magnitudes that we
can never avoid. We know that it is impossible to express love toward
all without exclusivity, for example (see [The exclusivity of love](#h:d37d7dd6-c2cc-4c7c-9332-5fe1263219e6)).
This is precisely what Atropos symbolises: the one and only way that a
given situation can exist. Atropos (&laquo;no tropos&raquo;, &laquo;no [other] way&raquo;) is
death, Atropos is also man&rsquo;s daily life within limits.

We speak of the three fates so as not to end up in extremes. We do not
accept absolute free will, but we also reject the complete absence of
will.

# Embodied mind

Our religion requires worshippers to be responsible so that they
operate, individually and collectively, within limits. Consequently,
our religiosity is expressed through deeds. We reject every school of
thought that disrespects the body and assigns importance only to the
spirit. We are not spiritualists, because we recognise our nature as
embodied mind.

We know that if we go beyond the limits, we will suffer the
consequences in the here-and-now of this world. We do not wait to see
the outcomes in some other life or realm. Perhaps there are lives and
generations beyond the world we know. They are not, however, the focus
of our thought, for at present we must concentrate on the life we
have, on the data of our condition, with the capabilities granted to
us. In other words, we consider human as-is, not other idealised
entities.

We emphasise responsibility because this way each person knows that
they cannot escape the situations created by their exaggerations. No
one will receive special treatment, nor will anyone be exempt from the
repercussions of their irresponsibility. One will suffer in this world
and/or harm the people around them.

Our religion is based on the recognition of the existing human under
everyday conditions of life. We are not interested in thought
experiments that do not lead to practical conclusions and sustainable
behaviours. For us, the embodied mind is aesthetically captured as the
healthy body and glorified through athletic contests, such as the
Olympic Games (see [The symbolism of the Olympic Games](#h:300dcc96-5b3d-4eb0-8c1b-3aed11fb3211)).

We know that a common fever is enough to prevent us from thinking
clearly or acting at the peak of our abilities. Consequently, any
lifestyle that harms the body, such as consumption of alcohol, drugs,
and junk food, necessarily corrupts the spirit.

We know that a healthy body enables and assists sound thinking. A
healthy person has the capacity to impose their self in their space so
as to enforce a regime of safety for love to prevail (see [The exclusivity of love](#h:d37d7dd6-c2cc-4c7c-9332-5fe1263219e6)).

We feel no shame for our body, as we regard it as the current home of
our spirit. Nature ordained that we be an embodied mind for reasons we
do not know and which, in any case, we cannot change. We cannot escape
this framework to live, say, solely as disembodied spirits. Our body
has needs and generates feelings, all of which contribute to the
multifacetedness of human existence.

For us, the body is the temple of the soul. We respect it, keep it
clean, and keep every degenerative element away from it. We are
polytheists because we know that the common, and the multitude, and
the relation between them all exist (see [Triadicity](#h:86390a41-0e58-4514-8bc3-05ade8068870)). Within ourselves
we find all elements of the cosmos, such as wrath and serenity,
violence and ingenuity, and the rest, as a communion, as parts of a
singular whole, the many through the one and the one through the many.

Human is not mind alone. We do not privilege thought as superior to
other aspects of human nature. We accept emotion as well as the awe of
aesthetics. For this reason, our religion accepts all people, the
philosopher, the mason, the doctor, the artist, and others, as equals
and equally useful to the common good, always guided by the
perpetuation of our kind.

# The symbolism of the Olympic Games

[ Translation note: in Greek we use the term &laquo;agons&raquo; instead of
  &laquo;games&raquo;, which is best translated as &laquo;struggles&raquo; in the sense of
  &laquo;hard work&raquo;. This better describes what sport is about, anyway, but
  I will go with &laquo;games&raquo; here. ]

The sin of exaggeration exists potentially in everything we do. Each
of our actions and initiatives can lead to extremes that, in one way
or another, harm us (see [The sin of exaggeration](#h:9df22656-3512-41dc-b9ce-e12d4fedc51c)). Consequently, we
must remain vigilant so that we can maintain moderation as the
interplay of factors changes.

Our festivities include athletic contests because through them we can
best convey the significance of daily struggle. The athlete must
dedicate themselves to their craft. Every day they must train and
remain ready for the moment of competition. They cannot remain
complacent, become lazy, and withdraw into the world of
irresponsibility, for then they will not be ready to compete.

Contests take place at regular intervals to emphasise how essential
consistency of effort is over time. An athlete is one who remains true
to their work. We celebrate this human quality because it is
beneficial for serving the common good. We want each of us to give our
best self in relationships with other people. This is precisely what
we call &laquo;philotimo&raquo; (φιλότιμο), the honour we have when we fulfil our
duty to the best of our abilities.

All of us carry out our own struggle in life. We symbolise it through
athletics, but we know it pertains to every role in all phases of
daily life. We do not undervalue any endeavour. Every person who
strives to raise their level even by a single point already has the
right mindset.

We encourage all members of our community to express their best
selves. We criticise those who rationalise their laziness through
contempt. We denounce their idleness because we know that this
exaggeration of theirs will bring us evil, decline, idolatry, and
ultimately ruin (see [We reject miracles and idolatry](#h:5b5e051d-eb86-4898-adf0-9fc384511a8d)).

# Triadicity

We understand good and evil as gradations of the factors in the given
case which are arranged in a certain constitution (see [The sin of exaggeration](#h:9df22656-3512-41dc-b9ce-e12d4fedc51c)).
We speak of relations and correlations that are always triadic.

To grasp this meaning, we will turn our attention to the simplified
individual. Human acts. Actions have consequences. Hence we already
identify two entities: human and the consequences of their actions
(see [The Fates of chance, choice, and the inevitable](#h:60cf69e5-76bb-4535-b742-215a16e2ff07)). From the one
(human), we moved to the two. Yet human can never escape their
actions. The world those create frames and determines future actions.
Consequently, from the one we have two which stand in relation to each
other, and thus they are three. The three exist by way of analysis,
but in practice they are always one: there is no human without
actions, no actions without an agent, no relationship without at least
two parts.

Triadicity can be understood as body and soul in relationship with
each other, and thus as embodied mind (see [Embodied mind](#h:8c2c7bd6-870c-44c1-8d42-7b4aed8383d2)). It can also
be understood as the transcendence of thought, the particularity of
instantiation, and the relation between them. The same applies to the
universal, the particular, and their mutual relation, such as the
triangle as such and the specific triangle that anyone can draw. The
same holds also for human and their community, human and limits, human
and the gods, always in their mutual relationship.

Religion itself is also a manifestation of triadicity, such as
individuals, their values, and the relationship between them. We
emphasise triadicity to underline the importance of relationships and
correlations. We do not speak of good and evil as abstract entities
because, as stated, we know that anything which can be good can also
be evil when, in a specific situation, its presence is excessive. If
we do not think in terms of triadicity, we fail to understand
relationships and thus recognise neither good nor evil.

# Each person to their craft

Religion is a collective affair. All societies distribute the total
work among their members. Each person specialises in their craft and
contributes to the common good.

Society needs tutelary figures at every level: from the extended
family, to the school, to local self-governance, to the religious
hierarchy. Individuals ought to know their role in each case. Let the
priest concern themselves with rituals and the organisation of the
worshippers, without aspiring to be a censor of the arts. Let the
artist engage in their art without pretending to be an expert on every
subject. Let the politician look after keeping the nation strong
without imagining they are omniscient.

Our relationships, therefore, are based on mutual respect. This, in
turn, is the result of self-knowledge in relation to where we stand.
We aim for collectivity and strive for everything we do to be
sustainable, i.e. to endure over time.

We express solidarity among ourselves and across generations because
we know that, first, everyone struggles to remain within the limits,
and, second, our actions, whether individual or collective, always
