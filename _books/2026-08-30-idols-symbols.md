---
title: "About idols"
excerpt: "An essay on idols, indirectness, and truth."
---

The idol is a reflection. In Greek, from where we get the English
word, we say that "I am staring at my idol in the mirror" («βλέπω το
είδωλο μου στον καθρέπτη»). The idol is a representation: it consists
in indirectness.

When we think of something as a false idol, we are either referring to
an inaccurate representation or to an untrue subject.

The former case can happen if, say, I purport to show a selfie of me
which turns out to be a picture of one of my dogs. The idol is false
insofar as it does not represent Protesilaos. But as a representation
it is true in its indirectness and it is also true in accurately
representing my dog. More generally, it is accurately describing
something even if it is not that which is supposed to be represented.
For example, an abstract painting of me may not be accurate the way a
photograph is, though it can be accurate in expressing a feeling in a
manner that the photograph cannot. Or it can even be accurate in
showing what expression can be made manifest.

The latter case of the untrue subject is more nuanced. Suppose I show
you a sketch of philosophyman whom I consider the one true
philosopher. You may argue that philosophyman is sheer fantasy or some
fallen angel or, anyhow, not the one true philosopher. If "untrue
subject" means that there is no person who is philosophyman, then we
are focusing on one specific interpretation of the representation.
Philosophyman may not be referring to a person, however: it can be an
abstraction, such as a set of qualities that are found in all
philosophers hitherto. Philosophers think things through and so,
philosophyman must be the most intense thinker. To think things
through, one must be patient. Philosophyman is thus the most patient.
If philosophers are indeed thinking things through and are patient,
then philosophyman is an accurate representation of the philosopher as
such, though not of any one person in particular. And if this is
inaccurate, then we are back to the previous point.

The only way for the subject of a representation to be considered
untrue is for that very subject to be impossible. Though what does it
even mean to have a representation of that which is impossible? If it
is impossible, there is nothing that can be predicated on it, as it
does not occur. If it does occur, then it is possible qua occurrence.
For example, I cannot say "nothingness is beautiful" because the very
conception of "nothingness" is already reified in my mind as
"somethingness", which then acquires the quality "beautiful". I hold,
then, that strictly speaking anything that can be represented cannot
be false in the sense of being impossible.

How about the representation of an imaginary being then? Suppose we
have a blue dragon on the mountains of Cyprus that types on the
keyboard with telekinesis. Such a being does not exist in my area, I
promise. Yet in describing this dragon, I have already rendered it as
a representation of a thought. It already is a figure of the thinkable
and, ipso facto, it always was in the potential of the thinkable. You
can picture it in your head now, using your imagination and you can
add your own qualities to it. The being is not real, in the sense that
we cannot go out there and meet it, but it also is real, actually and
potentially, in the sense that it exists and can exist as thought.

Now blue dragons may seem far-fetched, so let me use a practical
example. A program I imagined is not real in the sense that I cannot
go find it somewhere out there. Before implementing it, it is a
thought or series of thoughts, if you will. These thoughts are real:
they did happen. Furthermore, these thoughts can create material
consequences through their application. Through the program I connect
the thoughts in my head to material consequences, realised through the
computer.

In broader terms, every plan that is carried out from idea to
application is a program, already in the literal sense of that which
is "pre-written" (πρόγραμμα, προ (prior), γράμμα (letter or that which
is written)). The house I built existed before I built it, though not
as materials pieced together but as a clear plan of all the disparate
pieces coalescing around an organising principle, which gives us an
organism by the way.

At the time before the program is implemented, we can say that it is
not real in the sense that there is no instantiation of it beside what
is in one’s mind. Here, "real" is understood as matter. Though, again,
it would be real in the sense of being a mental presence and thus of
having the potential to become or otherwise contribute to something
other than the idea itself.

There is no untrue subject thus far.

False idols are then understood as an alias for false gods: people are
worshipping something that is not true. This, however, requires a
definitive notion of truth. For example, I may think that money is a
false god and, by extension, people who worship money are doing
something wrong because money is not the highest value in the world.
"False idol", here, does not make money unreal nor the worship of it
and all derivative patterns of behaviour: those are happening. The
appeal to "false" then is not literal. It is a shorthand way of
formulating an exhortation such as "there are higher values, and there
is the value which is the highest of all, so worshipping the lower
value as the only one that matters disqualifies other values and thus
makes people not think of them, which in turn conditions their
behaviour in a manner that causes longer-term problems".

I could keep elaborating on this exhortation, though what I wrote is
already enough to demonstrate that the phrase "false idol" is itself
an idol, for it too consists in indirectness. We have a short phrase
which we employ instead of the much more verbose statement. The
compact form thus symbolises the expanded form. Idols are symbols and
symbols are idols. We can use the words interchangeably, unless we
focus narrowly on the meaning of "reflection in the mirror", as I
noted above (though even that is pictorial, else symbolic).

Consider again the reflection in the mirror. A reflection of me is a
symbol of me in the sense that it is a facet of the entire world that
I am: it does not have feelings, for instance, which means that it
does not have my multifacetedness. If I show you a selfie of myself, I
am showing you an idol which points to the actual person but which
nevertheless has a life of its own. The idol is its own presence. When
you check the picture of someone you have never met, the idol still
engenders in you a reaction: you might for example find the person
attractive and you might further imagine that this person is polite as
well as funny. We then notice how idols are as real as a punch in the
face: they too can produce material consequences.

That which produces an effect is real at least insofar as it exhibits
the capacity to form part of the cosmic structure by which cause leads
to effect, effect leads to cause. The idol of me is of the world as
much as I am. The difference between us is one of degree: I am more
complex.

Now think again about money as a false idol. We realise that it too,
qua idol, is as real as you and me. Dismissing it as "false" is a
figure of speech, for we cannot affect its state, as aforementioned.

Which brings us to the exhortation against the false idol; an
exhortation which itself employs an idol, else a symbol. The
exhortation rests on the notion of an absolute value and, thus, of a
ranking among values. For there to be a ranking, there has to be
something that is common to all values which we can discern. The
relationship between the lower values and the absolute value must then
be one of reflection, else representation, else one facet versus
multifacetedness. All the selfies of me and all the words used to
describe me are symbols of me, yet neither individually nor
collectively do they exhaust me and nor do they amount to me as such.
The same for values: the lower values are idols of the absolute value
insofar as they consist in indirectness.

Can we have directness? Can it be absolute? Can we even have it for
the banal case of a personal relation? Do you know anyone fully? Do
you even know yourself fully? To know one person fully means to know
not only its actuality but also its potentiality. If I know myself
fully, then I must know what each of my constituents is and to also
know it fully. I have to have full knowledge of every cell and of
every process within it and between them. And so on. Factoring in the
potential, I must know it fully, since if I learn something new about
myself through experience, then I necessarily did not know myself
fully. This potential extends to all of my constituents in isolation
and in their interplay.

If I know myself fully, I am still not dealing with directness, as
there is knowledge about something. To be direct, the knowledge of the
thing would be the thing. As such, the potential me would be the
actual me. The future me would be the present me. You understand this
is no longer a human being.

The absolute value, which we may describe as ineffable, essence as
such, supreme God, or whatever we choose, can only be known through a
representation. The reason is that to know the absolute absolutely we
too have to have knowledge of its potential. In other words, we have
to be omniscient. Though knowledge which is absolute must coincide
with presence which is absolute, for it cannot be about something else
or outside or, anyhow, indirect. Thus omniscience is ubiquity. And
absolute ubiquity demands that there is no cause for it, as that is
indirectness, so it always is. There is no before it, no after it, no
outside of it, no it and something else.

To not belabour the point I am making, here is the gist: those who
fight you on the premise that they are against your false idols do not
realise that idols is all we have in a state of indirectness.
