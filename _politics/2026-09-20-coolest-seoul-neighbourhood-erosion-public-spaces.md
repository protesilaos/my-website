Writing for _The Guardian_, Raphael Rashid comments on the degradation
of local culture [in a neighbourhood of Seoul](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/sep/19/i-live-in-the-coolest-neighbourhood-in-the-world-its-not-as-great-as-youd-think), South Korea:

> A wave of tourists and visitors has changed the character of the
> Seoul neighbourhood I live in and helped create a place that no
> longer feels like home

Raphael's story is one that many around the world can relate to. It is
especially true across much of Southern Europe, including Cyprus where
I live.

## The environment of individualism and distrust

Gentrification happening to a megacity like Seoul is part of the
process. Cities of this scale are fundamentally not geared towards
community. The more they grow, the more alienating they become. This
is not because of some malevolent scheme at keeping people disunited:
it is a side-effect of scale combined with pace.

If we think of a city in abstract terms, we are dealing with a highly
dynamic system. People come and go from anywhere. Their connections
are not strong enough to prevent them from moving. They do not stay in
one place for a long time. They are rootless. This is due to economic
activity, which typically is some kind of service or office job.

Offices can be relocated and employees may work from a distance, for
example. Even when the workplace remains fixed, the employee may still
relocate to another part of the city where rent is more affordable or
some other benefits have become present. In this context, it is common
to not know one's neighbours within the same building, let alone the
entire block.

Each person is thus reduced to an individual or to a small group like
the atomic family. They may still have friends, but getting to meet
them typically requires arrangements that are indistinguishable from
formal appointment on the agenda. By this I mean that they are the
product of concerted action: they do not happen organically without
coordination within a given locality. More so when individuals
relocate within the city's boundaries while keeping contact with their
old friends from another part of the city.

Combined with the high population count, the individual becomes
nameless and invisible. Socialisation is yet another job. If they have
a small group of trusted contacts, they can still schedule events with
them if salaried work permits, but are otherwise operating as a
stranger among strangers.

This is an environment that breeds distrust. If an individual starts
greeting passers by on the street, for example, they will be treated
with suspicion. And rightly so, given the circumstances, because
nobody knows who that person is, what is their background, and what
may their motives be. Same idea for neighbours that come and go: they
cannot be relied upon.

## The process of alienation

The urban environment forges the individualist. Each person realises
that they have to fight for themselves or their tiny group against
practically everybody else. They must rely on their own means because
there is no wider community, no milieu built on trust and solidarity,
to fall back to.

Profit thus becomes the singular pursuit of each person and the
currency that regulates social relations. Gentrification follows
naturally. There will be no restraint in radically altering the
character of a neighbourhood because there are no emotional ties
involved: it is just another transaction.

Though even without the gentrification of neighbourhoods, there is
rampant alienation. With distrust as the backdrop of human relations,
the public space becomes increasingly narrower until it is rendered
obsolete. It cannot be trusted. Parents, for example, will typically
not leave their children unsupervised. They will prefer to keep them
confined to four walls, where they can "safely" melt their brain on
the computer all day, than go run around carefree.

Adults face a similar challenge. The elderly are easy prey for
criminals while women are correctly cautious to move around,
especially at night. As gangs become more prevalent, even the toughest
men become easy targets, because no amount of physical strength can
counter machetes and firearms.

When the public space is not undermined by distrust it is eroded by
profiteering. To socialise, each person must effectively pay for
everything. These days a mere coffee is a non-trivial expense. Those
who are on a tight budget are thus faced with the very real prospect
of not socialising as much as they would like to: they are steadily
priced out of their humanity.

Couched in those terms, the individual eventually realises that they
do not matter. Nobody cares for them. They are estranged from what
they thought was their milieu because their milieu was always
essentially individualistic and thus not "theirs" in any meaningful
way. It was all a lie.

## The illusion of choice

The megacity is an environment of caution and fear counterbalanced or
otherwise obfuscated by a false sense of ample opportunity.

Adults may choose the city life on the premise that they can seize one
of the many opportunities it provides. They believe that there are so
many activities they can pursue and places they can socialise in.
There are shopping malls, cafeterias, restaurants, theatres, stadia,
and more.

Each of those, however, is a for-profit business which means that
one's options are a function of their disposable income. The
incentive, then, is to maximise profits, which brings us back to the
aforementioned individualism. And when everybody thinks the same way,
eventually no amount of profit is enough for the average person.

What practically happens to the city-dweller is that they live with
the illusion of choice while settling on a relatively simple routine.
Work takes over their lifeworld, as they need that extra profit to
keep up with the demands of city life. Their day is spent in commuting
and on the job, after which they are too tired to do much else.

The money-centric organisation of society is a pay-to-win scheme writ
large that alienates most people while rewarding a tiny minority of
ruthless profiteers.

The lack of connection each person has is encapsulated in the fake
plants they keep in their room. That plastic cactus on the shelf
symbolises the city life they have accepted as normal and desirable.

## Mass tourism and ethnicide

[ I have also written about this topic in [When your country is a theme park](https://protesilaos.com/politics/2025-09-02-when-your-country-is-a-theme-park/) (2025-09-02). ]

Make no mistake: this is not specific to Seoul. I am merely using that
article from _The Guardian_ as a pretext for a broader commentary.
What I am describing herein is the ordinary experience of the city as
such.

The few times I visited that god-forsaken place called "Athens,
Greece" I wanted to leave as quickly as possible. Yes, the Parthenon
is still there but Athena, the goddess of wisdom, is long gone because
the underlying ethos that instituted her as sacrosanct left the moment
money was deified. Today's Athenians love you if you pay well in one
of the many pretentious areas or otherwise treat you like a mangy
vagabond.

Mass tourism is the bane of our nations. It warps the minds of the
locals towards profit-seeking at the expense of local communities.
Every random fellow now peddles short-term rental services. They
charge a small fortune for a weekend's stay. The locals are priced out
of the market, pushed to the margins of civilisation, and placed on a
fast track towards oblivion. Once urban regions are gone, rural areas
also fall prey to this omnivorous appetite for money.

This is how I ended up building my simple house in the mountains, for
example: accommodation fees not only became exorbitant over the span
of a few years but house owners would not even let me in since they
prefer short-term rentals to faceless foreigners.

Alienation effectively means that you are not welcome in your own
country. You are treated with contempt and derision. Your only way
back to a pretence of normality is through money: you buy amiability
and are liked for as long as you play that game.

Mass tourism and its flip-side of gentrification are killing our
ethnic identity, i.e. ethnicide. Displacement means that we no longer
have the chance to form strong connections which, in turn, leads to a
diminution in cultural output of a specific ethnic kind. Whatever
comes off as local culture is a streamlined simulacrum of so-called
"tradition" that exaggerates superficialities in order to entertain
stereotypes and cater to the ignorance of tourists.

The nations of the money-god are not content with ruining their
densely populated areas. They are doing the same to the entirety of
their territory either by selling it off outright to so-called
"foreign direct investment" or turning it into a tourist attraction
which hates true locals.

In my land, Cyprus, the whole island is being converted into a theme
park for the parvenu to live their escapism carefree. All streets are
made to look the same. Ancient villages are supposed to follow
official directives for what supposedly "traditional" houses look
like. It is a shadow play for the nouveau riche to pretend they are
gritty village folk when they visit their third house on that one
summer weekend.

Previous generations worked with what they could gather from the land.
They built tiny houses with shared walls in order to cope with
poverty. A large part of the village population would gather to help
with construction work. This was a dwelling that was set up adjacent
to the person's relatives. Everybody was involved as a community. They
were not following some smug bureaucrat's inane guidelines; guidelines
which, by the by, cost a fortune to comply with and thus have the
effect of shifting property to the hands of ever fewer people.

The big bureaucrat and the big moneyman love each other. This is what
capitalism is in practice: state intervention in favour of the capital
holders; state intervention against community. It consists in the
symbiosis of concentrated money and concentrated power, which
culminates in gigantism: the propensity to accumulate ever-more
resources at the centre against all expression of locality.

Within the context of the aforementioned, gigantism grows in strength
because it faces no pushback from local communities. Once everybody is
reduced to an individual, they can be dispersed like leaves in the
wind. It is just a matter of time before that happens. Whereas those
who are tied to their land, their extended family, and their
neighbours of several generations, are not going anywhere without a
fight. They have roots. They are like the reeds, which might bend but
remain in place no matter how heavy the storm is.

What the journalist Raphael Rashid describes in _The Guardian_ is true
though is still too polite, as a boutique view of events, to give the
proper weight to these phenomena. I hope my language acts as an early
alarm mechanism.

There is no reversing this trend by doing more of the same. Unless
people change mindset to live a frugal life and to prioritise
community within the confines of locality, the money-god will turn us
all into sacrificial lambs.
