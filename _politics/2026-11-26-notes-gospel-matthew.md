---
title: "Political notes on the Gospel of Matthew"
excerpt: "Political commentary on the Gospel of Matthew from the New Testament."
---

This is a comment on politics.

In the Gospel of Matthew we have a story of infighting among the Jews.
Jesus is culturally Jewish as are his disciples. For example, "Petros"
(Peter), whose name is derived from the Greek word for "stone"
(πέτρα), actually is Simon the son of Jonah (Matthew 16:17-18).
However, among the disciples are Andreas (Andrew) and Philippos
(Philip), both of which are Greek names without an explicit Jewish
alternative (Matthew 10:2-3). The name "Andreas" is in reference to
the Greek word for courage (ανδρεία) while "Philippos" means "friend
of horses" (e.g. the father of Alexandros was Philippos). More on
these ethnic details below.

Jesus is meant to fulfil the prophecies as the Jewish Messiah. He
speaks within the context of that tradition through all sorts of
references (e.g. Matthew 5:17-18, 22:36-40), goes to synagogues
(Matthew 9:35, 12:9, 13:54), and observes Passover/Pesach (πάσχα),
i.e. the celebration of the escape of the Jews from Egypt (Matthew
26:2, 26:17-19). That Pesach came to mean something entirely different
for Christians is beside the point, as that happened after the events
described in this Gospel.

Jesus appears as a teacher and is explicitly addressed with the title
"rabbi" (Matthew 26:25, 26:49; though check his comment about "rabbi"
in 23:7-8). While on the cross, he asks in the vernacular why the
Father has forsaken him (Matthew 27:46). The Greek original actually
explains the words of that tongue into Greek.

Jesus' salient points run counter to the status quo. He argues
forcefully against the religious authorities as well as the mainstream
religious culture (Matthew 15:1-9, 23:1-36). Those are misled to value
money/mammon (Matthew 6:24, 23:16-17) and are acting in pretentious
ways (Matthew 6:1-18, 23:5-7, 23:25-28).

In this Gospel there are hints about the character of Jesus. Those
include a loving and compassionate side (Matthew 9:36, 11:28-30,
14:14, 15:32, 20:34) though there also are clear instances of what I
consider his stringent attitude. He is willing to be confrontational,
such as in the aforementioned opposition to the authorities. He is
also forceful and decisive in his actions, such as in how he curses
and withers a fig tree that did not bear fruit (Matthew 21:18-20).

The movement of Jesus is fundamentalist in substance, reformist in
outlook, and counter-cultural in organisation. It aims at redefining
the Jewish religion and, by extension, the Jewish kingdom. The sole
authority is Jesus who demands absolute submission to his project
(Matthew 10:37-39, 23:8-10, 28:18). Nevertheless, Jesus also treats
his followers as siblings/brothers (Matthew 12:48-50, 23:8, 28:10),
meaning that Jesus is elevated above all people as their master while
seeing all of his followers as equal among themselves.

The conflict with the existing power structures is direct and leaves
no space for compromise (Matthew 12:30-32, 15:12-14, 21:33-46). The
establishment is naturally against such revolutionary tendencies and
proceeds to eliminate Jesus (Matthew 12:14, 26:3-4, 27:1-2). This is
an internal affair among the Jews. The Romans, representing the empire
which has suzerainty over Judea, cooperate with the Jewish authorities
in order to suppress potential civic unrest (Matthew 27:11-26).

Once it is clear that the Jewish leadership and mainstream resist the
reformist initiative (Matthew 21:43, 23:37-38, 27:25), the final
direction is to globalise the message (Matthew 28:19-20). Though
leading up to that pivot, Jesus is already not completely excluding
non-Jews/Gentiles (Matthew 8:5-13, 15:21-28).

Why globalise what essentially is a Jewish sect? Why is there such a
sect to begin with?

From my study of politics I find that religious differences strongly
correlate with ethnic particularities and reflect localised
socioeconomic realities. Is this the case with the radical movement of
Jesus? Did the Jews have second-class citizens among their ranks? Were
there, for example, converts to Judaism or subjects of their state
whose ethnic backdrop or even racial characteristics pushed them to
the margins of that society? We can think of plenty of examples in
ancient as well as modern societies where people of different
ethnic/racial backgrounds were/are, in one way or another,
marginalised, such that social inequality overlaps or directly links
to ethnicity/race.

Ethnicity is key. Matthew does not cover the lineage of Maria, the
mother of Jesus. He does, however, go to great lengths to present
Joseph's genealogy, linking him to Abraham and David (Matthew 1:1-16).
Joseph's own bloodline is irrelevant though, since he is not supposed
to be the father of Jesus. Maria is found pregnant by the Holy Spirit
(Matthew 1:18). Joseph adopts the baby by naming him Jesus (Matthew
1:21, 1:25), though that does not, _ipso facto_, make Jesus a
descendant of Abraham and David and, therefore, Matthew 1:1 about
Jesus Christ as son of David and son of Abraham is true only in a
conventional sense.

Can we surmise that Jesus was not, in fact, of Jewish lineage? Could
he have sprung from a milieu of Judaised people in "Galilee of the
Gentiles" (Matthew 4:15)? Could those people be second-class or
marginalised within their society and were they, as a result, seeking
ways to move towards the societal centre? Is it possible that their
communities retained in their oral traditions or even secret societies
the memory of their own ancestry and non-Jewish values? In the Greek
original "gentiles" are "έθνη" ("έθνος" singular, from where we get
"ethnic"). Which were those ethnicities, given the history of those
lands? It is at least probable that Greeks were among them, as well as
other peoples of the wider region.

Even the case of Hellenistic Jews is worth considering in this light:
what were the particularised and potentially multifaceted social or
ethnic reasons for whatever Hellenisation they underwent and how
exactly did they distance themselves, if they did and/or to the extent
that they did by eventually converting to Christianity, from the main
corpus of Jewish society? Was the presence of Hellenised Jews among
the ranks of early Christians a sign that those people were never
first-class Jewish people? Alternatively, did they have other
motivations or projects they were working towards to globalise the
Jewish tradition?

Combinations of these and others are possible as well. At any rate, I
will not elaborate on those matters and speculate on all the
possibilities herein. I am, nonetheless, stating my current suspicion
that Christianity already from its inception could have originated as
an outright political, anti-establishment and thus anti-Judaic and, by
extension, anti-Roman programme, albeit with the distinct possibility
that it was always meant to retain its unambiguously Jewish character
while making expansionist advances towards all other nations.

Even if Jesus and his movement started out as reformists and
fundamentalists who were solely interested in the house of Israel
(Matthew 10:6), the Christian tradition that developed afterwards
deviated from Judaism as it drew from Hellenistic thought as well as
elements of culture that were prevalent in the Near East and beyond.
Indeed, we can find instances of deviation from established norms
already in the words of Jesus, such as his rejection of _lex talionis_
(Matthew 5:38-39) and his strong opposition to the moneyman ethos in
general (Matthew 6:19-24, 21:12-13).

I shall elaborate further in other notes on the New Testament.
