This is a note on politics.

In the Acts of the Apostles from the New Testament we learn a great
deal more about what I covered in my commentary [from yesterday on the Gospel of Matthew](https://protesilaos.com/politics/2026-11-26-notes-gospel-matthew/).
Namely, Jesus and his followers were fundamentalists who sought to
renew and to co-opt the Jewish faith while claiming to fulfil its
existing teachings. They worked within the framework of the Jewish
tradition, taught at synagogues, invoked the Jewish scriptures, and
generally appealed to a messianic tradition (e.g. Acts 2:14–36,
17:2–3). This sect was seeking to take over from the existing power
structures within Jewish society. It openly confronted the authorities
(Acts 4:1–22, 5:17–42). As such, the conflict between the custodians
of the existing faith and the zealots of the new way started out as an
internal affair among the Jewish people.

Underlying this religious tension are matters of ethnicity and
socioeconomic conditions. Already from the Gospels we learn about
Galilee and understand the distinction between Jews and Gentiles. The
latter are the ethnicities that do not belong to the house of Israel.
As I noted in my previous article, I suspect that the society of that
time had among its ranks second-class citizens. Those could be so
because of their mixed ethnic background, provincial origins, and
relevant social standing.

The hotbed of Jesus' movement was in "Galilee of the Gentiles"
(Matthew 4:15), which comprised populations that had been Judaised
over preceding generations and which were at times looked down upon by
the Judean elite in Jerusalem (e.g. Acts 2:7, 4:13). The religious
fervour they brought was their vehicle for upward social mobility: to
challenge their elites and to take control of the realm.

Their gambit eventually failed in its immediate political aim. Jesus
was captured and executed, forcing the strategy to change. What
started out as a local sect for Jewish renewal took on a globalist
mission: to spread the same message of salvation across the Roman
world, underpinned by the familiar Jewish prophetic narratives.

The Acts of the Apostles is telling us that this pivot initially
remained Jewish in spirit and was embedded in interpersonal Jewish
networks. Thus, I use "globalist" to refer to the drawing of linkages
between the various Jewish communities across the Roman Empire: I am
not suggesting that this was some global community of unrelated
strangers which appeared spontaneously out of nowhere.

There are numerous references to the Apostles heading straight to the
synagogues upon arrival in each city (Acts 13:5, 13:14, 14:1, 17:1–2,
17:10, 18:4, 19:8). Those early Christians did not immediately abandon
their heritage (Acts 18:18, 21:20–26). They rather performed a
tactical shift to broaden the geography and demographic scope of the
conflict with the mainstream Jewish establishment, eventually turning
toward Gentiles and so-called "God-fearers" when the established
synagogue leaders rejected them (Acts 13:46–47, 18:6, 28:28).

Those "God-fearers" existed at the periphery of Jewish communities and
I shall thus describe them as part of a "peri-Jewish" class of
enablers and opportunists. We can surmise they were people that had
life activities around synagogues, mingled with Jews, and, perhaps,
were hoping to gain some advantage by converting to Judaism. The new
way was effectively offering them a conduit towards equality in the
kingdom of God as "sons of Abraham" by faith alone, completely
bypassing the requirement of circumcision and other Jewish customs.

We learn about events that happened in Cyprus, for example (Acts
13:4–12). The Apostles did not preach indiscriminately to all the
locals. Instead, they visited the Jewish synagogues in Salamis (Acts
13:5), and then journeyed across the island to Paphos, where they
engaged not ordinary populaces but a Roman politician alongside a
Jewish court magus (Acts 13:6–12). Those who know the landscape
understand that the Apostles travelled from the eastern side of this
otherwise large island to the western parts in order to, as we already
know, leverage existing Jewish networks and, through them, reach
influential political patrons. At any rate, they were not addressing
street crowds.

The same pattern unfolds in Syria, such as Antioch, where the
disciples were first called Christians and where debate took place
over the status of Gentiles (Acts 11:19–26, 15:1–2). Damascus was no
different. That is where Paul was initially sent to arrest disciples
of the new way in synagogues (Acts 9:1–2) and where he began preaching
immediately after his conversion (Acts 9:19–22). It continues through
Asia Minor towards the Greek mainland through Philippi (Acts 16:13),
Thessaloniki (Acts 17:1–4), and Beroea (Acts 17:10–12). Again, the
target audience consists of Jews and peri-Jews (Acts 17:4, 17:12,
17:17, 18:4). There were thriving Jewish communities in all those
places, which the Apostles visited and to which Paul sent his famous
letters.

To recapitulate and then elaborate on my commentary:

- Jesus and his followers were Jews, fundamentalists, and reformists;
- there is ambiguity about the ethnic composition of Jesus' movement,
  with what I speculate is a mixed ancestry that creates social
  realities of marginalisation as well as an incentive to relax the
  criteria for admission into the faith;
- the movement of Jesus is rejected by the mainstream Jewish society
  on doctrinal grounds, though we can always think of this as the
  ethnic core successfully resisting an incursion from its immediate
  multi-ethnic periphery;
- the movement of Jesus turns globalist by immediately leveraging the
  Jewish communities across the Roman Empire;
- the original ethnic admixture becomes relevant once again as
  Gentiles, else peri-Jewish people, are accepted into the newly
  emerging faith.

The numbers of a given faith are bolstered through (i) marriages which
lead to conversions and offspring that is immersed in the culture,
(ii) voluntary conversions that happen because of a change of beliefs
or sheer opportunism as with the peri-Jews, and (iii) forced
assimilation. In a sufficiently complex political order, all of these
will be in effect.

Converts to any ideology tend to exhibit excessive zeal and lack of
restraint. Those of diverse ethnic/cultural backgrounds can experience
marginalisation and feel they are misfits wherever they go (though one
can be a misfit for other reasons as well). These two tendencies in
tandem produce powerful incentives for realignments in terms of social
organisation. Those affected seek to elevate their social standing,
challenge established legal-institutional arrangements, and take
authority into their own hands.

Central to this state of affairs is a certain kind of person which
manifests as the Hellenised Jew, the Judaised Gentile, and related.
These are people who are effectively caught between worlds. They are
not actually Greek, yet not Jewish enough. That sort of tension seldom
is reconcilable in reference to established norms. This class of
people is the product of successive empires that ruled over the lands
that, roughly speaking, exist between Greece and Afghanistan, Egypt
and the Caucasus.

Where there is travel, trade, and lots of people with mixed cultural
backgrounds involved there are newly forming beliefs that draw from
older traditions while creating something entirely novel. For example,
we have the phenomenon of "Greco-Buddhism": two views on quotidian
life that simply cannot work as one, yet the ruling elites have a
strong incentive to cultivate such fancies in order to control their
subjects while retaining a simulacrum of their heritage.

"Hodge-podge" is perhaps the best way to describe beliefs that take
form in those conditions. A good proxy is the modern Western person.
Owning to globalisation, they adore Korean pop music, admire the way
of the Samurai yet buy everything just-in-time, practice Buddhist
mindfulness while deciding to stay in the big city, dabble in Peruvian
ayahuasca and cosplay as indigenous people for a week before returning
to their urban hellhole, love Jesus though they are not Christians but
still believe in God without further details, have only good things to
say about the Sufi mystics but are hardly pressed to name five cities
across the Middle East, claim that Greece is the birthplace of their
culture, and so on. Resilient communities do not take form under these
conditions, though spaces are formed in which the opportunist---and
increasingly the opportunist with the attention span of a
goldfish---thrives.

What is attractive and what stands the test of time are the people who
do not chase the latest fad. It is those who stick to their way of
life and resist every upstart's advances. Christianity did indeed
start strongly but lost momentum until it eventually got taken over by
the emperors. It promised miracles galore at the outset and thus
created the precedent and the expectation for countless examples of
abuse and concomitant idolatry that persist to our day.

There is no going back once you tell people that magicians and special
items, from stones, to bones, to icons, and more, will be fixing their
problems. To me, the lessons I have learnt from the study of history,
politics, social dynamics, religion, and human nature are the
following. They work in concert and must always be taken together.

1. **The core of the faith has to be elitist and absolutist.** No
   miracles, no faith healers, no mumbo-jumbo, and thus not
   everybody's wishes/hopes are relevant. There are strict rules.
   Mystics/initiates act as custodians of the community. They have
   spent their life thinking through all the relevant issues. As such,
   there is adherence to the hierarchy within the community, which is
   how you get respect for every tutelary figure, starting with family
   roles (indeed "hier-archy" literally means "holy" and "rule").
2. **Resist becoming a vehicle for social mobility.** Everyone is
   welcome but only after they understand what they are buying into.
   Nobody gets preferential treatment and there shall be no shortcuts
   whatsoever. Otherwise you are providing incentives to the
   unscrupulous "God-fearers" of this world to come in with the hope
   of gaining some easy win. This is extra tempting if you are also
   open to miracles, because then everyone is a hipster and a
   magician.
3. **Austerity keeps the arivists away.** There must be no signs of
   opulence anywhere. Gifts or so-called "sacrifices" have to be
   symbolic in nature and preferably cheap. The cheaper it looks, the
   better it is because people may then focus on the substance without
   getting distracted by social status. As soon as you adorn it with
   gold, you feed the monster in the human's heart.
