title: "On the trade-conquest continuum and the rise of statecraft"

---

In previous essays, I have written about the trade-conquest continuum
in relation to European politics and geopolitical considerations with
respect to other parts of Eurasia. I want to use this space to expound
on the theory at some greater length. This is the beginning of an
inquiry into matters of statecraft broadly understood.

## Emergence of the power apparatus

To understand the world, we have to break down its complexity into
intelligible chunks of simplicity. We have to design models that help
us reason about the core mechanics of a give state of affairs. Then we
can add more factors to our study to make it better approximate
reality.

To this end, we have to think of trade in its simplest form: a
transmission between two points. It is, in its most essential
formulation, indistinguishable from communication. There are at least
two parties and there is a medium through which they come into
contact. Thus, we have the two parties and the relationship between
them, which in the case of trade we call the transaction. The two
which stand in relation to each other form a triad. The triad is
singular in its conception, though we see that it actually is two
which exist in a relationship, thus three.

The only way that a transaction can happen is if there is transmission
of signals between the two points. For there to be communication from
point A to point B, the space between them has to allow the signals to
be transmitted, in order to establish the feedback loop which in the
case of trade is the exchange itself.

Add people to this consideration now. You will have individuals
engaging in a transaction and they will be going from one place to
another in order to carry out trade. We are thinking of trade routes
or markets more broadly.

Suppose there is no security whatsoever. A merchant starts from point
A and has to go to point B where they can sell their goods. At point B
they will enter a market. For this business to be profitable or,
indeed, possible, the trade route itself must not be blocked. Just as
with a communication signal, its transmission must not be interrupted
or intercepted.

Assume that all people are benevolent. There are no thieves and no
criminals. Everybody abides by the law and follows the same moral
code. Basically, it is a society of angels. Even then, there are other
dangers along the way. There are large predators, venomous snakes,
physical hazards... These are all variables that can change the
original plans of those involved in trade.

Variables beget uncertainty. If the merchant does not believe the
route to be safe, they will conclude that the venture is very risky or
not viable at all. Trade thus collapses. Trust is a prerequisite.
Those involved need to reduce their exposure to the vicissitudes of
the world, else to control as many factors as possible. They will want
to deter animals from interfering with the trade routes. They will
seek to build infrastructure in place that makes it more safe to
travel. They will desire, in other words, to create conditions that
provide for a modicum of safety or more, such that there can be trust
in the business endeavour as a whole.

We know, however, that people are not benevolent and that we are not
angels in any meaningful sense. We understand very well that even
though there will be benevolent actors, there will also be those who
are inclined towards criminality, who would rather make profit without
putting in the hard work, hoping that they will get away with
impunity. In effect, such people have to be kept in check.

There thus arises a natural requirement for security. Security
precedes trade and, indeed, all figments of civilisation. For there to
be trade, there has to be the space within which a transaction can
happen. And that space has to be secured from intervening forces and
defilers, whether those are animals or other people.

Even in an anarchic conception of trade, there must be certain
conventions or rules of engagement as well as corrective mechanisms
that will prevent mischief and misbehaviour. Those will instil in
everyone involved a sense of trust that transactions can happen as
intended and that all relevant agreements will be fulfilled on the
delivery of goods, the provision of services, and so on.

Continuing with this anarchic view of trade, we already notice that
there exists a baseline of agreement. Anarchism, even when it is
construed in individualist terms, has to start out as collectivism and
will forever consist in collectivism. It cannot be any other way, for
if it is that, then there will be those aforementioned intervening
forces that introduce variables which can destabilise the space and
thus break the trust in its viability.

Strictly speaking, then, there is no such thing as anarchy. There is
always an _archy_ (αρχή), in both senses of the original Greek word:
(i) a beginning from where things start to happen as well as (ii) a
power apparatus that organises certain states of affairs in specific
ways. The archy has this dual role vis-à-vis trade and will eventually
have it for all other phenomena, be it directly or indirectly.

In its simplest form, the archy is a convention among free-willing
individuals. That fits the typical view of libertarian anarchism.
Though it still is a power apparatus. It enables certain patterns of
behaviour and will necessarily have to dissuade others. Failure to do
so will eventually destroy the archy, as discord and then uncertainty
will prevail.

The group governed by an archy is thus functionally equivalent to an
organism: the parts organise towards an overarching objective. Once
the parts move irreversibly in their own direction, or their
containing space is overrun by outside forces, the organism is no
more. Thinking that human affairs are somehow different than the rest
of the world is to misunderstand who we are and under which conditions
we subsist.

For there to be an order, there are rules. For there to be rules,
there are criteria of conditionality attached to the attainment of
stated and tacit ends. Conditionality has to be credible. Those
involved must believe that there will be consequences either way,
whether they conform with the rules or not. Absent credibility there
will eventually be absence of compliance with the rules which, in
turn, will bring about the disintegration of the given order.

## Non-violence is predicated on the capacity for violence

The very presence of rules means that there is a shared incentive in
the potential use of force. Individuals whose actions run counter to
the collective interest, to the common good, will be punished. In
other words, an individual's rights cannot be inalienable in the
strictest sense of the term because a collective will have power over
those individuals in one way or another, even if that power is limited
by convention. Whatever inalienability of rights consists in agreement
which itself is collectivist. This too is a matter of convention,
again pointing to the presence and workings of the archy.

A society may agree on a minimally sufficient corpus of views. For
example, "do not do to others what you do not wish to happen to you".
That is a good starting point, but not enough. There must also be
agreement on how to handle disagreements and how to interpret cases
which are not clear-cut. Indeed, anyone who has thought seriously
about non-trivial problems understands that there is no such thing as
"clear-cut" and that interpretation is always involved. So who gets to
do the interpretation, within which boundaries, and to what extent,
must all be figured out, among other issues.

This is where property rights or anyhow a convention of ownership is
established. Such is not a matter of money and wealth per se, though
it relates to those as well. It rather concerns the traceability and
attributability of consequences back to agents of action. Reward and
punishment are predicated on such an understanding.

In their simplest form, rules can be construed without actual violence
involved. We can think of consenting adults engaging in transactions.
We can imagine cooperating people who conduct the affairs of their
neighbourhood in a loving manner. And we can generally fathom people
who have a pro-social outlook and will always be eager to arrive at
some kind of agreement with everybody involved.

Though there will always be cases where disagreements arise even among
benevolent people, due to how the logic of reward and punishment
unfolds. Should someone intervene to help a person at the risk of
homelessness? Should that intervention happen even when the person in
question is reckless with their money, not willing to work, a thug and
a bully, et cetera? At what point does the benevolence of others stop?
There has to be a terminus. For different people, the limits will
differ as well, hence diversity of opinion, and thus a wider surface
area for possible disagreements to unfold and for opinions to be
reconciled through some agreed upon mechanism.

Disagreements will be about the distribution of resources, what should
be done to cope with immediate problems, how best to prepare for
future challenges, who assumes responsibility, and so on. There will
eventually have to be some conflict resolution mechanism, such as a
system of courts of law, as well as a decision-making framework, i.e.
a political process. In all cases, credibility is contingent on
enforceability: whatever the courts or the political body decides has
to be enforced even if that must be done against the private interests
of some members of society. Again, inalienable rights cannot exist in
the strictest sense of the term.

Even in a state of non-violence, there always is the potential for
violence, which is what grants ultimate credibility and enforceability
to any given collectivist deliberation. Violence is the backstop.

I have already hinted at how the potential for violence exists against
other forms of life. It is wielded against large predators, poisonous
snakes, toxic plants, ticks and disease of all kinds, or, generally,
any force that will try to enter the space of humans to potentially
undo its security underpinnings.

How do you deter wolves from attacking the flock of sheep, for
example? One way which does not involve the complete annihilation of
wolves is to have sheepdogs around, which themselves are robust and
aggressive in order to deter their wild cousins. The sheepdogs can be
considered a humane way to handle this threat, though "humane" is a
euphemism that does nothing to change the dynamic: the potential for
violence is there and so is the readiness to use it.

I am not talking about the necessary actuality of violence, but only
the fact that it can happen given the right circumstances. The same is
true for a fence, a house, the borders of a nation, boundaries of any
kind: absent deterrents, they do not exist.

Wherever non-violence occurs then, it is predicated on violence. It is
made possible by some originary violence that carved the safe space
out of the wilderness and continues to exist within the framework of
potential violence.

## From the local to the global

A safe space is a controlled environment. It follows that for there to
be safety, the relevant factors must be held constant or within a
predictable and tolerable range of possibilities. This creates a
certain totalising logic; a logic whereby factors that keep changing
must be controlled and rendered less variable.

Think of a peaceful town in the middle of nowhere. People there have
established their covenant. They manage their affairs through
non-violent means of exchange and communication. There still is this
potentiality for violence, as I have already explained, but otherwise
they just mind their business and everything is nice.

Now, suppose that there is an invading force. It is a group of
marauders that has come from somewhere far away and is about to
pillage this town. What do the townsfolk do? They have to fend for
themselves. They must collectively defend the town and thus the common
good. They will fight as a unit against this invading force, else risk
getting killed, raped, enslaved, et cetera.

Suppose now that they stop this incursion: the invaders were not
prepared for a fight and were caught by surprise. Nobody was killed in
the end. Still, what is the immediate incentive the townsfolk have? To
boost their defences, of course! They do that in the hope of never
having to live the same stressful day again. And what if the invaders
keep coming back? This town will eventually want to bolster its
numbers and increase its power to not become prey. Eventually, they
will wish to snuff out the danger at its source by counterattacking
the invaders.

In order to cope with the challenges, therefore, an otherwise peaceful
community will have to become war-capable, even if it is not
war-mongering, per se. It has to preserve its common good. This is the
organism in action: it has a sense of self-preservation.

This town may not be willing to engage in conquest. They have no
interest in that. They are peace-loving people. They just wish to live
their own life and not interfere with the affairs of others. However,
for them to stem the tide of successive waves of invasion, they will
want to conquer the invaders or somehow do trade with them.

Trade always becomes a form of expansionism. This is because there has
to be concordance of spirit, i.e. shared values, for there to be
business accords. Those invaders will have to learn ways of behaviour
that make them trustworthy in the eyes of the townsfolk. One of the
sides involved will eventually impose its worldview on the other or
anyhow there will emerge a new set of values as a synthesis of some
sort which shall supersede the existing values. Whatever the case
though, there cannot be disagreement at that foundational level. Else
there is distrust, lack of credibility, and ultimately no trade at
all.

## The microscopic underpinnings of imperialism

Against this backdrop, we understand how trade becomes an expression
of culture. It codifies a certain normative view of the world about
how things ought to be. This must, at the very least, govern the
transactions involved. Eventually, though, once those are streamlined,
the cultural dimension of trade will grow to cover group dynamics, the
organisation of labour including such seemingly trivial matters as
working hours, the distribution of resources, the ordering of
priorities, and so on.

Once we extend these principles, we are able to realise that what
starts out as a localist understanding of the world has to become more
globalising and eventually globalist in outlook. It is all about
controlling the factors that constitute the case in order to limit the
scope of variability and thus to engender faith in the mechanisms of
transaction.

The globalising tendency becomes apparent once we consider the
consumption patterns of a modern individual who has sufficient
disposable income. Picture someone who lives in a city. They do not
produce their own food and/or do not get it directly from the
wilderness either, in the form of foraging or hunting. They have to
buy everything from the market. Similarly, this person does not
generate any or even some of the energy they require on a day-by-day
basis. In other words, their life is entirely dependent on trade and
on the predictability I outlined above.

This lifestyle is literally impossible if there is no predictability.
One cannot rely on a network of trade which usually fails to function
and which is not robust to shocks. The moment there is a failure in
one part of an interconnected system, all the parts will collapse and
thus this person's own life is in jeopardy. To put it in simple but
clear terms, if truck drivers in any given modern country stop
working, the country will collapse: no food supplies, no health
provisions, no delivery of energy sources... Nothing!

The totalising logic then of trade is not to be misconstrued or
misunderstood as a matter of sheer greed, even when there clearly are
greedy people involved at every level. Rather, the totalisation of
control is a product of doing trade and the precondition for more
trade to be done. It is a prerequisite for people to put their faith
in a network of relationships, all of which are underpinned by
concordance of spirit. For those involved to have faith that their
lives will not be forfeit, some power apparatus has to control all the
relevant factors.

Imperialism is the propensity of a people to extend their reach beyond
the narrow confines of their own milieu. Every group of people that
engages in trade has built into their commercial endeavours the
propensity for imperialism, owning to the mechanics I have already
explained. This includes clearly unscrupulous governments, like that
of the United States of America, as well as those who present
themselves as reluctant hegemons, like the European Union in its
pre-jingoist phase (e.g. early 2000s) and modern China.

In the present day, the connection between an individual's spending
choices and the global infrastructure is salient. For example, a
person who uses AI to do their work. AI is produced by some company.
The AI provider depends on the energy grid. That, in turn, requires
the procurement and processing of raw materials that function as
inputs to power the data centres, thus more trade agreements all with
their potential violence. It also relies on ongoing maintenance work
from relevant providers. All of them need security guarantees for
their expensive investments. Hence private guards, police forces, fire
departments, the army, intelligence agencies, international alliances,
and much more. Without belabouring this point, the individual's
lifestyle is inextricably bound up together with the established order
they are immersed in.

## The dubious notion of a global "win-win" situation

Already from the introductory materials in economics, we learn that
trade enlarges the pie. Those involved will get to share in the
wealth. More trade means greater enrichment for everybody. The exact
distribution of wealth is no longer important. The idea is that even
if there is inequality within a country, such that there are people
who are very rich and those who earn the minimum wage, the tide will
still raise all the boats so that even the poorest in that society
will actually be quite affluent in absolute terms.

The theory is that trade makes everybody better off. This, however, is
not a historical account of events. It is a boutique view that
conveniently leaves out a very important facet of the trade-conquest
continuum and what has been happening hitherto: the reality of war
and, in particular, the workings of colonialism, dominion over other
countries, looting of resources, the funnelling of wealth from distant
lands into the coffers of the imperial metropolis, and anyhow the
geographic redistribution of profits through either actual or latent
force. Inequality within countries and between countries are
interlinked.

There is no trade without the potential for conquest. All routes have
to be secured. This means that the ships which carry goods are
accompanied by the ships which are outfitted with guns. The places
where commerce can happen, such as at ports, are also the places where
armies will want to secure access to. Those who make an investment
have a vested interest in its success. And when we are talking about
big money, we have accordingly big interests, which involve equally
big bombs. In its purest form, such is the ultimatum: do trade with us
or be conquered by us. It fundamentally is about the imposition of
control, be it through vassalage, colonialism, or even ethnic
cleansing.

Only when we describe things in such plain and at times disturbing
language can we make sense of the history of humankind. It is
impossible to appreciate events, from the affairs of primitive people
to modern society, if we are thinking of humans as benevolent agents
and of cases of destruction as anomalies caused solely by greedy
elites. That is not a sufficient explanation because it does not
account for the stratification of interests that goes from the top to
the bottom in which even the most deprived in society have an interest
in the continuation of the collective project.

Those at the lower parts of the income distribution stop thinking of
the collectivist project, and of imperialism in particular, as their
ally when they no longer believe that they are its net beneficiaries.
For as long as they assume that the power apparatus is their own,
inseparable from their country, and thus their irreducible common
good, they will acquiesce to whatever happens to them and to others.

There is, finally, something to be said about the notion of shared
prosperity. What if we all agree to be average in some globalist
order? Do we then liberate ourselves from conflict altogether? I think
not because we still do not control all the variables. If, say, the
people in the other country do something stupid to harm themselves,
does the rest of the planet become poorer willingly to keep everybody
equal? What if some resist such a course of action? Which mechanism
will enforce the collective will? And why would that mechanism,
virtually omnipotent as it would doubtless be in such a scenario, not
gradually promote its own interests? My point is that conflicts will
persist in every form of political organisation because the world is
unstable and people are diverse.

It is laudable to work towards peaceful coexistence, to prioritise
diplomacy, and to exercise maximum restraint when it comes to the use
of force. It is nevertheless naive to expect there to be no incentives
for domination. We cannot escape from the mechanics of the
trade-conquest continuum. Those will inform our actions and underpin
our reality, whether we want it or not.
