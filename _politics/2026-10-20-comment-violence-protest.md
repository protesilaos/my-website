---
title: "Comment on violent protests"
excerpt: "A general view on political violence."
---

I was asked about my opinion on the violent protests in France. I do
not cover day-to-day events because the details seldom are necessary
for my theoretical insights. What follows is a general view on
political violence, governance, and reform.

## Political violence as a tool

Violence is a tool. It can be used by the forces seeking political
reform. It may also be employed by the establishment. In both cases,
what matters is the application: how the tool is used and in pursuit
of which ends. To dismiss it as such is to pretend that it can never
be useful.

When the 18th century French revolutionaries were beheading members of
the Ancien Régime, for example, they were effectively issuing a
corrective against profligacy. They were delivering justice. That this
was carried out by gruesome means is important insofar as it made the
message unambiguous.

The same tool of political violence can, of course, be used to uphold
an otherwise abusive status quo. Violence thus performs a function of
unleashing certain forces while containing others. What the goal is,
is notionally distinct and can be reasoned about as such.

Violent protests will amount to something more than sheer destruction
if they are supported by programmatic action. If there is a concrete
follow-up programme that can be implemented to usher in some kind of
thoroughgoing change, then violence shall contribute constructively to
the refashioning of the state.

If, however, such programmatic action is absent or present yet
tokenistic, then violent protests will eventually degenerate into
collective mindlessness; into anger that fuels unrest and facilitates
looting with no higher end in sight.

## Popular does not always mean good

Protests always have concrete demands, at least initially. They are
mindful in this regard. Though demands themselves do not yield a
specific programme. It is not enough to know what one wants. One must
also have a clear idea of how to get it and, above all, be prepared to
make sacrifices in their own life where necessary. Otherwise, it is
wishful thinking at best and reckless governance at worst.

This may not matter to the teenagers who protest, for example, though
it still is significant for politics at-large. The state, or anyhow
the agent of collective action, must be in a position to realise the
given reforms, which themselves need to be defined in clear terms.

There can be no progress without a well-designed agenda that connects
desires to results.

That granted, governments do not always do what their people want. One
reason is corruption. Another is legitimate longer-term planning. For
example, people may ask for cheap credit to finance their everyday
spending. Doing so is not free of longer-term consequences, even if it
costs virtually nothing for the central bank to print oodles of money.
A responsible leader has to factor in the implications and decide for
the general good not just in present time, but also for the future.

One area where this is desirable is ecology. A typical policy along
those lines involves some present day inconvenience, however minor, in
exchange for greater longer-term benefits for people and the other
species of this planet. There are cases where there are no
inconveniences to be had, though the principle is that certain
policies can be rationally understood as broadly beneficial for the
long-term even if they are unpopular at the outset.

Put differently, the popularity of any given idea does not, ipso
facto, make it benign.

Shrewd and unscrupulous governments will try to put a positive spin on
their stratagems, such that those qualify as elements of responsible
statecraft. Though this does not change the fact that a policy can be
popular and still harmful.

## Understanding the contradictions

Politics is complex because it is replete with trade-offs and bundles
of mutually exclusive choices. One has to factor in the prerequisites
as well as the foreseeable consequences.

Which brings me to common contradictions that we see everyday. For
example, people in Europe will complain about how expensive life is
right now. But they will also be against Russia, which supplied them
with cheap energy. And they will not be willing to make deals with
Iran, which can also give them cheap energy. And they do not like the
Chinese, so they make Chinese exports extra expensive. And so on.

Politicians want to be popular. Same with influencers and other
prominent figures. So nobody ever says the unpleasant truth out loud,
which leaves the philosopher in the awkward position of being the
cynic: there are inescapable trade-offs in life and you cannot have it
all. If you want the opulent life in which you live beyond your means
care-free, you have to accept alliances with people you do not like
and/or you have to support your own country's imperialistic
propensities. Those riches you dream of will not just come out of
positive vibes.

It is very easy to condemn imperialism. It is much more difficult to
lead a lifestyle that, once generalised, renders imperialism surplus
to requirements.

## Withholding judgement

I choose to let events unfold and judge the results. People have
demands I agree with. I think governments in Europe have grown
complacent and are increasingly unaccountable. It is time to make them
realise that things cannot continue this way.

Though I will not celebrate before the match is over. The protesters
must prove that they know what they want and are able to enact it as
the new order. They have to couch their ambitions in terms of a wider
programme that can deliver sustainable results. Perhaps they can do
so, though what I have seen thus far makes me cautious.
