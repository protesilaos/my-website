---
title: "Hierarchy in the extended family"
excerpt: "Comment on some of the benefits of having an extended family."
---

This is an excerpt from my journal.

* * *

Some distant relatives visited me recently. I had not seen one of my
elder cousins in roughly two decades, though I had kept in contact
with the others. Cousin remarked that I have not changed at all. I
happen to do a clean shave of my facial hair these days, which makes
me look younger, so that story checks out. Me being a smartass around
loved ones, I noted that I actually do not age: "I just gain experience".

Cousin told me about their children. One of them is already an adult.
I was aware of it but was still mildly surprised to piece together the
facts. To my mind, a 20-year-old was born in the mid-90s. I guess this
is what the stable mountain life does to you: you remain fresh, but
you also lose sense of the years flying by.

I commented how I am eager to meet everybody and we will make that
happen soon. My longer-term ambition is to reintegrate the family,
buttress it with new neighbours, and rekindle the values that we
almost lost to consecutive systemic crises.

With those and other relatives, we all agree on the importance of the
extended family, which is a feature of traditional Greek culture. To
which I emphasised that in terms of familial ties I do not do degrees:
blood is blood.

The extended family has many benefits for individuals. The babies are
raised by all their cousins, uncles, aunts, and grandparents, so the
burden is not placed exclusively on the parents like it does in the
atomic family. Same principle for the elderly. And everybody has
people around to talk and do social activities with. This is a natural
unit of solidarity that has stood the test of time. It does not rely
on the state apparatus.

What the extended family also does is establish hierarchy and, by
extension, enforce conformity with rules. The elders teach and
demonstrate the values, while the rest embed those lessons into their
behaviour.

These structures can, of course, be unfair, though that is a matter of
how they are used. Hierarchy is not inherently tyrannical. The parents
have, for example, power and control of their baby child, though this
is used to help the child grow in a controlled environment until it is
ready to rely on its own means. Too much freedom too early will
definitely be catastrophic.

This is the essence of a virtuous hierarchy: to keep checks in place
for those who need them because, fundamentally, not all people at all
times know how best to exercise their freedom. Why that might be
depends on all sorts of factors. They may not yet have the skills.
They might be going through a tough period of emotional instability,
which clouds their judgement. They are the kind of person who thrives
when they get as much feedback as possible, but who otherwise
hesitates to take the initiative. Or they simply have not thought
things through to understand the consequences of their actions.

A right or obligation is balanced against the totality of rights and
obligations. There is no such thing as an absolute prescription in a
vacuum. Individual freedom, then, is not the only factor to be
accounted for. The longer-term wellness of the individual as well as
the group is equally important. For example, I will not sit around
twiddling my thumbs while a relative is doing heroin: I do not respect
your freedom to ruin yourself and to make your entire family suffer
forever.

There is this Western notion of "my body; my rules", which effectively
does not recognise the externalities of one's actions. When the junkie
ends up living a lifetime of psychoses, there no longer is "my body"
that is affected, but the bodies of everyone else. Lots of cases
follow this pattern, but I will not belabour the point.

This sense of the collective good extends to matters of honour. A
member of the extend family becoming a criminal is bad news for
everyone, so we all have a strong incentive to enforce the moral code.
We are not waiting for the bureaucrats in the capital to take action
after the fact: it is our duty to preserve what we value. This
arrangement creates the appropriate incentives.

Familial hierarchy is about love. Though love, too, is misconstrued
nowadays to mean "you are perfect as you are, sweetheart; do whatever
you want". This is a license for complacency, which breeds the kind of
feebleness that results in moral decay. Love needs to be tough
sometimes: a "no" builds character and serves you well long-term. At
other times, love will come in the form of emotional support and the
appropriate gentle touch. What matters is the wellness of the
individual through the coherence of the collective: the good of the
place and the good of the space, as I often put it in more abstract
terms.

Tough love is not tantamount to cruelty. It does not even involve
corporal punishment (I think corporal punishment typically reveals a
failure to lead by example or to be in control of the situation---put
differently, it is part of the problem). One is tough when they are
firm. And one is firm as well as equitable when they themselves embody
the rules they are enforcing.

Indeed, what undermines hierarchy is the elders not following their
own precepts. The uncle who tells you not to smoke while lighting up a
cigarette cannot show tough love: it is ineffective because of the
dissonance between words and deeds. What you need is the person who
does not smoke and who actually follows through with their words. That
is the toughest person around.

Your relatives calling out your nonsense in a spirit of love, or
helping you cope with a difficult situation in a way that is
empowering, is the best thing that can happen to you. They do it
because they care. As such, they are preparing you for the much
harsher world that is out there; a world in which certain strangers
will blithely throw you under the bus.

Cousin was an athlete and remains as fit as ever. This is one of the
embodied values I have in mind. If cousin comments on your habits, for
example, you have to at least listen because you know there is some
knowledge there that has been put to the test for decades. Thus
emerges the hierarchy that holds you accountable.

The elders in an extended family are not necessarily the oldest people
around. It is those who know what they are talking about and who
exhibit it as part of their life. You become a hierarch organically by
showing the requisite skill and responsibility. In practice, you are
there when others want your advice. This will correlate with age,
though age alone does not make you more prudent.

The extended family integrates friends and neighbours more easily. An
individual cannot necessarily do that. For example, I am friendly,
reliable, and talkative but I am also an introvert who tends to not
contact anybody (though I am actively working on changing this). The
integration continues naturally because, say, my friend may find
others in my family with whom to be friends with and to do interesting
things together.

As such friends and neighbours become de facto relatives and are
treated as equals. There is a sense of togetherness and belonging,
which builds solidity at the social level. I have experienced all of
this before and am eager to contribute to it at the earliest
opportunity. No matter the state of technology and how sophisticated
we think we are, the ancient traditions remain ever-relevant.

I was able to build a house here, with material and financial help
from locals and some people online. With my extended family around I
would have the potential to build an entire civilisation.
