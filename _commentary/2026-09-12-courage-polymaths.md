---
title: "The courage of the polymaths"
excerpt: "A comment on the character trait of the polymath and how it runs counter to bureaucratisation."
---

Writing for _Aeon_ magazine in an essay titled [Penchants of the polymaths](https://aeon.co/essays/four-islamic-polymaths-prove-breadth-beats-specialisation),
Mariam Sabri informs us about four geniuses of the Muslim world.

> This semester, my students are learning about four polymaths who
> shaped Islamic science: al-Biruni, Alhazen, Avicenna and
> al-Khwarizmi. Their circumstances could hardly have been more
> different: one wrote his way out of scarcity; one was held under
> house arrest for a decade; one distilled an era’s medical knowledge
> into a text that ruled European universities for 500 years; and the
> last worked at the largest research institution of his age. But each
> shared the same underlying instinct: to move freely between
> mathematics, medicine, astronomy, philosophy and poetry, testing one
> discipline’s assumptions against another’s. My classroom is in
> Pakistan, where students arrive eager to talk about disruption and
> employability, but rarely about breadth. I want them to see, through
> these four lives, that this kind of expansiveness isn’t a luxury for
> stable times; it has produced some of the most enduring
> breakthroughs in the history of science.

I wish Mariam good luck in inspiring a new generation of students to
follow in the footsteps of those remarkable thinkers.

What I would add to Mariam's account is the word "courage", which is a
necessary quality of character in keeping culture open to the
possibility of genius.

The polymath has courage in that they are prepared to move outside the
comfort zone of any given specialisation. They have the audacity to
draw linkages that others do not discern, not as a matter of superior
intellect but as a result of trying in earnest. By "polymath" I apply
the term in its original meaning, the one who has knowledge of many
things, without implying that such a person is necessarily a genius.

I write from a position of experience as a polymath myself. My skills
are well below those of the aforementioned geniuses. I have not
achieved anything remarkable in any of the fields I have delved into
and have no formal qualifications to even make a remotely plausible
case in my favour. Nobody references me now and none will remember me
in 500 years. I do, nonetheless, embody the courage and concomitant
exuberance that no promise of comfort can constrain and, as such, will
go wherever I want to learn whatever my condition renders possible.

I synthesise street smart with book smart. I know of politics in
theory as well as day-to-day practice. I have pierced into the psyche
of humans in sport and the classroom, in company and competition, in
solitude and in festivities, through hardship and opulence, in the
hinterlands as well as the centres of power. I combine prose with
poetry, rigour with beauty, intellectuality with physical prowess, a
tendency for collectivity with the reality of a lone wolf, a study of
human with an understanding of programming, an intuition on colour
with a conception of pattern as such into theology and religiosity.

What is common in this multitude is the virtue to give everything an
honest try by bringing to it the full power of my undivided attention.
It is to not seek excuses for inaction. I never say "I cannot" when
what that would really mean is "I do not want to".

My initiative is neither informed nor conditioned by the opinion of
others. They default to the kind of naysaying that confines a person
to a tiny room with no sight of the open vistas. Whereas I am not
satisfied with the comfy chair. I will hike to the top of the mountain
and dive in cold waters. I proceed with the belief that I can learn
anything I set my mind to and can perform admirably in it. Such is my
conviction that I am not afraid of the challenge.

This is not a matter of genius, which is beyond human design. It is
about attitude, which can be studied and greatly altered by continuous
effort with the appropriate method, even if it also has a biological
basis.

Genius is rare. I am not that person. I was not a child prodigy and am
not a late bloomer either. Though I can tell from history that genius
occurs more frequently and is clustered together in cultures that are
defined by openness. Those are the societies who are young in age and
spirit, freed from the burdens of institutionalist pretence and
attendant etiquette, and who exhibit that unmistakable zest which
drives forward their inquiry into the world.

Boundaries naturally occur in all fields of endeavour. There is a need
to maintain a trail of paper by which to track outcomes. It is then
followed up by the want to streamline processes, replicate results,
and create a baseline of expectations. The bureaucrat emerges
organically as an intermediary among those whose job it is to think
things through and/or make stuff happen. The bureaucrat starts out as
the custodian of records and eventually turns into the enforcer of
norms.

Scholars who had achieved something noteworthy at a certain point in
their career, and who lack the courage to press on with their inquiry,
wish to cling on to their holdings. They effectively set up barriers
to entry by weaponising bureaucracy in the name of standards, with
which they limit their competition and thus retain their privileged
status.

Over time, bureaucracy turns intellectuality into a pay-to-win scheme.
It confers social status and creates a sense of classism where the way
one talks communicates more about their perceived intellectual acumen
than what they actually say. At scale, this shadow play manifests into
a glorified popularity contest, more superficially sophisticated than
high-schoolers figuring out who is the coolest among them, though not
much different in function: the celebrated experts get the lion's
share of the money while the rest learn to be happy with leftovers.

Bureaucratisation evolves along with the fracturing of intellectuality
into increasingly narrower fields of specialisation. The bureaucrat
thrives in this environment as the driving force of the want for
interdisciplinarity, by becoming the interlocutor among the relevant
specialisations. The specialist, in turn, benefits from the resulting
obscurity. There is practically nobody who can check on the tangible
value of their contributions. This is a state of comfort, which can be
alluring, at which point complacency kicks in and the system as a
whole enters its state of decline.

Courage is both contagious and dangerous. When one speaks their mind
fearlessly, others are emboldened to do the same. Once a few do this,
the movement grows into a power impulse that emanates from the mind to
affect conduct, institution, and artefact alike.

The potency of courage consists in its unpredictability. The
courageous person will not accept to be constrained and is thus
inconvenient to any established order that finds comfort in stasis.

What teachers such as Mariam Sabri can do is instil a sense of
alertness in their students. Encourage them to continue where they
would otherwise stop. Celebrate their every attempt at formulating a
question and congratulate them for their eagerness to develop an
independent thought. Push them to go where their curiosity leads and
be there for them when they need a tutelary figure to help them
channel their vitality into a force of creativity.

This, however, demands that the teacher be not pedantic and obsessed
with adherence to formality. The teacher must, in effect, have that
spark of alertness to push back, even in subtle ways, against the
overreach of the bureaucratariat.

Do it right and the next genius might show up soon after to remind
humanity that the most predictable is the least animate.
