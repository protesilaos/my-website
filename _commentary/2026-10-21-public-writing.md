This is an entry from my journal.

* * *

I am an introvert. This means that I expend energy when I am in the
presence of other people and store energy when I am alone. Introverts
are often mistaken for being shy. While there is overlap, the two are
not the same. Specifically in my case, I am not shy: I mean exactly
what I say. I also am not touchy or sensitive and will appear
emotionally detached even when I am actively engaged in the given
activity.

Being sociable and having personal time is the right balance. I mostly
get sociability through my coaching work, the text correspondence I
maintain with various people I know or even strangers who ask me
various questions, video calls with friends and family, and the
occasional in-person event I attend. I wish the in-person events were
more frequent, but I cannot change that unilaterally. I am working on
it though and am open about making that a bigger part of my life.

My ambition is to help build a local community that consists of family
and friends. What is the point of being a one-man-army, like I am now,
if I cannot spontaneously tell my jokes to anyone?

Interpersonal relations, especially beyond tiny groups, are how we get
to participate in culture and how we may contribute to its making. To
cook, to dance, to do sports, and, yes, to argue and to fight. They
all have the effect of showing us something that is beyond our own
selfhood. We benefit exactly because we keep our horizons open; open
to the possibility of that which we are not; the possibility as
embodied in the other person; the "you", which is immanent to each and
every "you".

The community is not about maintaining an echo chamber. Variety and
indeed some pushback is benign and desirable, provided your
individuality has enough space to be expressed in. For me, this
practically means that I can still get some time alone.

Private time is restful even though I keep myself busy with tasks. For
example, I am now writing this article after having spent time on a
long video call with a friend. I do not feel burdened by the task. It
does not drain my energy nor does it deplete my willpower. By writing,
I draw immense fortitude from the heavens. My mind is razor sharp. My
emotions are stable. My poise is perfect.

This is all because of what I write about and the manner in which I do
it. Whether I expound on hotly contested political issues, general
philosophical themes, technicalities of programming, or artistic
considerations, I am always doing what I enjoy. I am not a "content
creator". I have not picked a niche on the basis of some business
calculation and then figured out what its audience wants to consume.
No. I speak my mind in earnest. I go as far as I need to and have no
interest if anyone is joining me.

I cover what I like. I do it as a means of personal fulfilment. My
ultimate goal is wellness. This is about my individual well-being,
which can then emanate outwards. For as long as I am in a good
condition, which is both physical and mental, others who interact with
me can also benefit. Either directly, since I will support them
through thick and thin, or indirectly by extracting the transferable
parts of what I do and then applying them to their life.

Writing is an outlet for my creativity. It gives me the boundless
mental space I need to put ideas to the test by thinking them through.
Whatever I write about is the distillation of intense thought that has
transpired over many sessions of meditation ("meditation" as in
"intense, focused thought"). By the time I write it, it is already
clear in my head and ready to be typed out.

Since the principal goal of my writing endeavours is to fulfil me, I
am not distracted by other considerations. For example, I do not keep
track of visitor data for my website. I do not care if anybody is
interested in my musings, let alone what exactly they are doing with
them. Similarly, I do not write for an audience. This is not some
grand people-pleasing project. I expound on controversial topics and
stand my ground with conviction. Not out of stubbornness, but as a
function of having thought things through, as demonstrated by my
nuanced statements.

Writing for my own sake means that I have found my lodestar. There is
a fixed position in the sky that empowers me to orient myself at all
times. This practically also means that I do not recognise any
obligation towards anyone. I cover whatever topic I am interested in,
say exactly what I think, and do it whenever I feel like. The entire
process is self-contained. As a result, I do not experience mental
fatigue, let alone burnout. The feedback I get from writing is enough
to keep me going from strength to strength.

The writer's life is the lonely life. There is no group event. You
experience it alone and, at some level, cannot share it with others.
It is very unlikely that you will meet a person who understands what
it feels like to be a writer and then even more unlikely to find
someone who also shares similar interests to yours. Put differently,
writing is the wrong tool if your goal is to make lots of friends.

I do not just write though. I also do it in public. Anyone who has
attempted this before knows the physical experience of stress that it
induces. Your heart rate is more pronounced, you have difficulty
breathing, and you feel pressure in your stomach. I often find that a
comment I responded to on YouTube is deleted shortly after I have
posted my reply. People yield to the pressure and eventually regret
voicing their opinion, even if they have not received any negative
response.

Imagine doing that over literally thousands of publications.
Fundamentally, this shows that I am not only happy in the fulfilment I
get from this activity, but also confident in my capacity to cope with
evolving states of affairs. My ideas are well considered, so I am not
afraid of being exposed. I put them out there exactly to increase the
difficulty, because otherwise I find it too easy.

For me, that which is too easy eventually begets complacency which in
turn results in enfeeblement. If it is a project for life, I prefer to
remain alert, potent, and dangerous. By putting my thoughts out there,
I am creating an accountability structure. I have set a standard and I
am making it harder for myself to find excuses not to live up to it.
Yes, this is my competitiveness in action. I thrive in the challenge.

Writing is difficult in itself. You have to keep a clear head to
collect your thoughts on the topics you are about to expound on. You
also need to be in a relatively good physical condition. If you are
too tired, for example, your attention will shift away from the text.
If you are suffering from a cold, you will not be in a position to
concentrate. And so on. The environment is equally important. If there
are too many things going on, all of which demand your attention, you
will simply not have the energy to commit to the task of writing. Same
idea for the computer setup: it has to empower you to focus on the
task. Consistent writing is a reflection of consistency in general,
which in turn speaks of attentiveness and decisiveness.

The feeling of being scrutinised is especially intense if you are not
clear that you are writing for yourself. If there is hope in the back
of your head that you will win accolades, friends, etc. then the fear
of being judged and of failing will quickly take over. If you do not
receive any comments, for example, you will interpret that as a sign
that you did something wrong, or that you are stupid, or whatnot. The
want for validation will grow stronger and will eventually compel you
to some seek some cheap win where you trawl for positive responses.
Those are a drug. It is where creativity goes when it wants to die.

There is no problem with public scrutiny if your attitude is right. If
you are writing for your fulfilment and if you are, in this spirit of
honesty, thinking things through, then there is nothing to be afraid
or ashamed of: you already have far greater mental fortitude than the
average critic. Indeed, to criticise demands knowledge of the subject
matter.

What we usually describe as "criticism" are poorly informed opinions,
half-examined thoughts, and momentary vibes. Only those who have gone
through the rigours have criticism. If you get such feedback, then you
should be dancing and singing because it will remake you for the
better. The actual critic can upgrade you by dissecting your project
and by telling you something about yourself that you are not aware of.
The rest are not telling you something about yourself, but only about
themselves with your publications as a pretext.

Public writing is a form of exposing who you are. Given enough words,
you cannot hide the flaws in your thinking and the errors in your
judgement. You cannot pretend to be somebody else. Even if you are
writing with an alias and not explaining anything about your private
life, a keen reader knows far more about you than you realise. I
already hinted at this with how consistency in writing points to
consistency in general and to other qualities by extension. The flow
of thought, the expansiveness of ideas, the coherence therein, the
interconnectedness of concepts... Those you reveal right away. Even
your character in terms of self-control, emotional regulation, and how
logical you are can be inferred. These are all reflected through the
text.

There is also scrutiny on what exactly you write about. You might make
a comment that someone finds insensitive. Or someone may think you
could say something else. Or they may not like your style. In short,
you might receive negative comments. If you keep getting those, it
ultimately means that you have to work on how you express your
thoughts and formulate your arguments. You can describe something
useful, for example, but do it by using a metaphor that is racist or
otherwise inappropriate. Nobody will think about how right you are,
but will instead point out your sloppy and ultimately ineffective
expository writing.

Inexperienced people tend to pay too much attention to negative
comments. Sometimes negativity points at something valid, but it may
also be a reflection of the person's own shortcomings. Learn to be
honest with yourself, magnanimous in your understanding, and fair in
your judgement. Does the other person actually make a valid point? Do
they say something that may be correct in another context but does not
apply specifically to you? Could it work for you as well? Can you
imagine changing your ways accordingly? Alternatively, you may find
that the comment you got shows a lack of understanding of what you
wrote: they did not engage with the argument, for example. Be fair in
that too and dismiss it without hesitation.

If you are not clear on your goals, negative comments will discourage
you from publishing. Are you doing it for the feedback? Why would you
pick this lonely and thankless task as your vehicle for receiving
validation? Negativity cannot touch you if you are not there for the
feedback. The same is true for positive comments though. Those can
also lead you astray because they may condition you to please the
audience in order to elicit another pleasant response. This is yet
another drug that will quickly throw you off balance.

Remember the ultimate "why" you are writing. Be faithful to that and
blot out all the noise. Then you will be untouchable.
