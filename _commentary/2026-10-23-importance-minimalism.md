This is an entry from my journal.

* * *

Minimalism seeks to approximate frugality in the face of abundance. It
works with the understanding that the human condition is optimised for
conditions of scarcity and is geared for a life of incessant struggle.
Where there is plenty, where there is no attendant incentive to be
sharp and alert, the likelihood of moral degeneracy is high.

Morality pertains to the features of a person's behavioural patterns.
It couches them in terms of normative outcomes, else standards that
are considered and are known to be benign for the individual and for
people at-large. In its most basic conception, morality is the
codification of the lifestyle that is frugal and rigorous.

That is so due to the formation of prescriptions on what to do, what
to avoid, and what to maintain as an attitude. In their essence, those
are rules for how to live well.

Morality is thus not exclusive to religiosity. Religion is an
effective tool for instilling morality. It is central to social
reproduction: to a society that perpetuates itself in both biological
and cultural terms. But religiosity is not an irreducible part of
being moral and, indeed, religion can be corrupted when it becomes a
business, a means of tribalism that is detached from the moral
imperatives that the society has in common, a mere instrument in the
hands of imperialism, and so on.

What must always be there is the spirit of restraint, of not yielding
to the plenty when they are available, and of remaining focused on the
longer-term demands of living.

This we all know without reference to some holy book or school of
philosophy. We experience it every day in the modern world. Some
examples:

* You can buy chocolate right now. Not just one, but as much as your
  budget can afford. There is more variety of sweet than you can eat
  on a single sitting. And the entirety of it is available on demand.
  Yield to it and you suffer. Not only will it bring disease, it will
  also make you sluggish. Sluggishness shall cloud your judgement.
  Your future choices will thus lack attentiveness to longer-term
  downsides. The ability to determine what is beneficial to you will
  be degraded. Which in turn will condition yourself to yet more
  mistakes that harm you. There is no friction, no difficulty in you
  getting the sugar, so no struggle, no sharpness, no alertness. It is
  a vicious cycle.
* You join social media, including those you think are more ethical
  due to their permissive copyright license or terms of service. They
  are equally bad for your mental health because they expose you to
  the abundance of information. Your sense of space and time is skewed
  until it is completely messed up. Events that are distant feel
  proximate. Dangers that are remote appear imminent. Yielding to the
  abundance of this kind of information will once again cloud your
  judgement, with all the disastrous consequences that
  always---always!---follow. Same idea for emotions that are decoupled
  from the friction of human interaction: you get oodles of them and,
  again, there is no effort involved, until the point where you are
  not really interested or inspired by them.
* You set up your computing environment to notify you about things
  that are happening. There are bells and whistles everywhere. Each
  part of the interface shouts for attention. In that environment
  there is an abundance of stimuli, without even accounting for the
  contents of what you are exposed to, which will further compound the
  problem. Yielding to this kind of plenty shall inevitably affect
  your attention span. You will lack focus, you will not be able to
  concentrate, you will feel disempowered and eventually jaded.

These are only a few of the many realities of modern life. The common
in their multitude is the reduction in friction. They make it very
easy to acquire that which is otherwise desirable. Food, friendship,
knowledge are all important to us. Though they are benign only
inasmuch as they are relatively difficult to get. They lead to
decadence as soon as the challenge is removed.

This is true even for trivial things. Think of how easy it is to drink
a fresh juice that consists of an apple, an orange, and a carrot. Try
eating those instead and you will notice that the task is much more
demanding. That extra difficulty is exactly what you need to not
overdo the consumption. Chew on a raw carrot and tell me if you can
have many of those. Then have a carrot juice and notice how many you
just consumed. When it is trivially easy to get the juice, you can
drink the equivalent of many fruits/vegetables, far more than what you
would have eaten. Chewing, then, is the friction you want in your life
so that it keeps you in check, notwithstanding other benefits it has.
Generalise those insights for everything and you already know how
dieting in a spirit of frictionlessness is deleterious.

It extends beyond goods that can be purchased or items that are anyhow
at your disposal. It covers expectations as well, for they too
engender behaviour that is akin to the aforementioned. The expectation
of ample choice, the sense of entitlement, shall already make you
sloppy or condition you to pursue ends that will lead to that
eventuality.

Friction forces you to focus. Scarcity keeps you honest with yourself
and true to the boundaries of your abilities. It continues to bring to
the centre of your conscience the moment of crisis ("crisis" is the
Greek word for "judgement"). You have to judge how to proceed, to
reason about the available options and make an educated guess of what
will be beneficial to you long-term.

It necessarily is about the longer-term because you are readily aware
of the fact that you will be living with the consequences of your
actions. Those who are decoupled from the results of their deeds are
dealing with the same problems as those who do not have enough
friction in their life. Indeed, consequences are "friction" in this
regard: they force you to think, they remind you of the limits.

A moral life, the actual living and embodiment of it, not virtue
signalling, is what maintains vitality. Think of what happens if you
are lazy: you are not trying to make things happen to the best of your
ability, so your brain is not firing on all cylinders, which means
that your body is not sufficiently active, your attention is not
channelled anywhere in particular, your energy dissipates, your mind
becomes blurry... All result in an experience that is in rapid
decline.

Same idea for every pattern of behaviour that is not consistent with
the rigours of struggle: it kills you before your time, it kills you
even if it keeps your body around by snuffing out that which is vital
in you.

Minimalism is not relevant in a life where scarcity is its defining
feature. People cannot overdo it willy-nilly even if they would
otherwise be inclined thus. But in a world that offers you everything
in spades, minimalism is how you begin to preserve your vitality.
