---
title: "Comment on tutelary figures"
excerpt: "A comment on what are tutelary figures and how they function in society."
---

I have written about the importance of tutelary figures in several
articles. Although it is clear from the context what I have in mind, I
have not elaborated in a standalone piece. A tutelary figure is a
person who watches over you while teaching you something that is
valuable for life. Parents typically perform this function, as do
teachers, coaches, and religious leaders, among others.

Every person does something we can learn from, including in the form
of an example to avoid. Though those people are not explicitly out
there trying to take care of you or, anyhow, contribute to your
long-term wellness. They are just minding their own business and you
learn by discerning the patterns therein. A tutelary figure is guided
by benevolence. They want you to succeed. This means that they can be
rough at times, if their position permits as much and the situation
calls for it.

A mistake some people make is to think that benevolence is the
attitude of accepting all missteps, of not defining boundaries, and of
effectively claiming that all numbers win. Sometimes openendedness is
the appropriate course of action. At others it does a disservice to
those involved because it does not give them the impetus and framework
they need to develop their capacities in ways that empower them to
succeed.

It can be disastrous to give someone exactly what they ask for,
because people do not always know what will cause them irreparable
damage. For example, giving too much freedom to a toddler is how that
child will harm itself. You do not let a 2-year-old walk to the beach
and go for a swim. It is not ready for that. Thus a parent who always
answers affirmatively is not being responsible.

Strict control is another case that is characterised by
irresponsibility, even if that is less obvious at the outset.
Continuing with the example of parenting, the beach-going parent who
never allows its child to learn how to swim is not giving enough space
for personal growth. The overprotected child may thus turn into an
insecure adult and/or entitled prick.

A tutelary figure understands that they are supposed to pass on the
baton or, anyhow, that they will only remain relevant in some other
capacity beyond what they were there for initially. Your parents will
not be babying you for long, though they might be there to help you
raise your own kids, and/or to keep you company. Teachers will expect
you to leave school and go do some job, including one where you are a
teacher yourself. They may still be your friends and chat with you
about all sorts of issues. Your coach will give you all the tools you
need to be able to do the task on your own. Then the coach can still
be there as your peer. The religious leader will allow the members of
the community to self-organise and still be present to share their
experience, to provide encouragement, and to correct any irregularities.

There are people who do not need a lot of protection and others who
benefit from closer control. People are diverse: one size does not fit
all. A child, for example, may already have the responsibility of an
adult at the age of 10, while another person will develop it in their
30s. The former is not necessarily better, because the 10-year-old
still needs experience, which it will only get in a positive way if it
is reminded that it still is a child. The latter case is not defective
or anyhow wrong. It too makes poignant the need for tutelary figures.

Tutelary figures hold authority. This authority can be organic, as in
the case of a family: the child starts out as a fully dependent,
symbiotic organism, that gradually learns from the adults around it.
Authority may also be ex officio, such as the teacher at school who
has the power to punish students with extra homework when they
misbehave. Though authority may also come from one's recognition of
excellence in another. If you ask someone for their opinion on some
important matter, you are effectively acknowledging that they have the
skills, knowledge, and experience to answer in a manner that is
helpful. If done with regularity, that person has authority over you
in this specific capacity.

Authority is an interpersonal phenomenon. It cannot stand on its own.
The teacher has no power other than brute force if the student does
not listen and if the institution does not grant them any remedial
action. The relationship is thus broken. Consequently, authority and
submission go together. One must submit to the other's guidance, which
is another way of saying that they agree to be handheld. Sometimes
this happens regardless of one's will, though it will typically be
subject to their acquiescence or express want. For example, the baby
has no choice in the matter, but other relationships do involve
consent, like one wanting to train under a specific football coach
during their free time.

Tutelary figures are involved in every stage of our life. If they are
not, then we must be our own best guide and guardian. Otherwise we are
lost and are in trouble. In effect, the tutelary figure is there to
show us what is a benign option and what would be detrimental to our
being. A person who falls into self-destructive habits, for example,
essentially does not have someone in their life who can put them on
the right path. The situation they are in sets them up for failure.
Perhaps they are lonely and/or stressed and/or overworked. There will
be a more descriptive explanation than merely "this person is bad".

Authority and submission is how hierarchies take form. Societies need
those to thrive. Individuals benefit from hierarchies at every stage
of their life. In the absence of hierarchy one must be stable in their
routines and clear in their thinking. Such is a rare case, however.
The vast majority of individuals in the vast majority of cases
benefits from guidance and control, which they can hopefully find in
their social milieu.
