---
title: "Why I do not consider myself a Youtuber"
excerpt: "Comment on why I do not monetise my YouTube and why I prefer creative freedom."
---

This is a topic that comes up from time to time, including in the
latest "Prot Asks" I did with Oliver: <https://protesilaos.com/prot-asks/2026-08-11-oliver-minimalism-distractions-independence/>.
Oliver wanted to say something and first checked with me if it was
okay to do so, given that it could demonetise the video. I noted that
I do not monetise my videos and that Oliver could speak freely.

I treat YouTube as a video hosting platform: it is always online and
does not charge me for the service. I know I am giving up other values
in exchange. This is a pragmatic consideration on my part.

I record a video whenever I feel like it. I do it for any topic I
like. I am not targeting a specific audience and am not optimising for
what people think or expect (same for all my publications, by the
way).

I am not motivated by Internet points, in the form of views, likes,
comments, subscriptions... There is no analytics software running on
my website and I do not have email subscriptions. My RSS feeds are
configured to show the full contents of the article, so that readers
do not even have to visit my website. I do not run advertisements and
am not doing paid promotions. In short, I have no idea who reads what,
unless they directly tell me about it.

I have nothing against people who use their website or YouTube channel
to sell products, track clicks, show advertisements, and so on. For
me, this is not a moral issue (and I do not consider myself "moral" or
even "good" in the conventional sense). Rather, it is a question of
creative freedom, which I value greatly.

I find that the pursuit of Internet points compromises your expressive
potential. You have the powerful incentive to do some or all of the
following:

- keep milking topics as much as possible, which diminishes the
  quality of each publication;
- employ clickbait techniques with misleading titles and thumbnails,
  among others;
- become a caricature of yourself, by saying things you do not really
  believe in just because of the expected virality/popularity they
  have;
- say what people want to hear, so you do not challenge them to apply
  judgement and to question what they otherwise take for granted.

What motivates me is to speak my mind in earnest. I do not want to
dilute my message with distractions. To my mind, the pursuit of
Internet points is one such distraction both when they are few and
when they are plenty. In the former case, you feel disappointed in the
results and become frustrated with your efforts. In the latter case,
you inevitably indulge in ego boosting, which is the petty man's drug.

Do not conflate popularity as such with vanity or shallowness though.
I am referring to the pursuit of popularity as the primary objective,
which is not the same thing as someone becoming known and respected
for what they do. For example, in the free software space, Richard
Stallman and Linus Torvalds are major figures because of their
significant contributions, which will stay with us long-term.

All of the above apply in varying degrees to social media in general.
Circa 2011 when I fist started my website, I created a Facebook page
for it where I would post links to my articles. It was effectively an
announcement board. I was getting a couple of "Like" reactions each
time. I initially thought they were innocuous little extras. Until one
day I wrote a long, fairly technical article that would require
several minutes to read and perhaps a few more to think through.
Within seconds of posting the link on the Facebook page, I got likes
and positive comments along the lines of "good stuff!" and "keep it
up!". I deleted the Facebook page shortly thereafter because I
understood how fake and shallow it was and how distracting it could
become. It took me a few more years to completely abandon social
media, including Twitter at the time, but the understanding was clear.

I do not count myself as a Youtuber because I recognise that
Youtubering has its own logic; a logic that I defy. Even when people
give me money, such as donations or for coaching, they understand that
they are not buying my consent or, indeed, anything that would
compromise my creativity.

If you want to succeed as a YouTuber, you do what is necessary. This
is a business approach, which is ultimately governed by the balance
sheet. And if you want to be maximally creative, you just do what you
enjoy. If there happens to be an audience for your musings, that is a
happy albeit irrelevant coincidence---and is a distraction if you let
it influence your decisions.
