---
title: "On the importance of rituals"
excerpt: "Rituals help us experience the slow-paced life that is necessary for understanding the world all around us."
---

I am not a genius. If you compare me to Leonardo Da Vinci, who was
elite at everything he did, I am not even good enough at all the
things I do. If you judge me relative to the skill of a Paco de Lucía
or the insight of an Isaac Newton, I am several leagues away from
them. Similarly, if you measure me against the technical acumen of the
likes of Alan Turing or Linus Torvalds, I fall short. My intelligence
is slightly above average, while the skills I have largely taught
myself are full of lacunae: they are only worthwhile if we charitably
apply the discount of "good enough for an autodidact".

Still, I do something very well that few people learn and fewer still
master: I slow things down, understand my surroundings, and am in
control of the situation. I have the capacity for situational
awareness that allows me to discern patterns and thus connections
which are otherwise obscure even though they are always present in
plain sight. This is not a matter of intelligence but of attitude.

This is because I have learnt through observation that perception is a
function of speed. The faster our pace in life, the narrower our field
of view, the fewer the details we can capture, the more superficial
the reality we will be experiencing.

Speed is not even a metaphor here. I mean it literally, though an
analogy helps communicate the point: while standing, you have a wide
field of view, but as you pick up pace the field of view becomes
increasingly narrow until its boundaries converge at a singular point.
Speed creates a tunnelling effect. If you go fast, you are thus not
seeing---literally "not seeing"---what is around you.

Life is high-paced when it does not allow for moments of mindfulness.
In the present era, this usually happens when we work under the
pressure to deliver results, log each second of the effort we put in,
capture every datum that empowers our employer to assess our
productivity, and do everything else beside our profession, including
relationships and recreational activities, as if it is a task on a
rigid timeline.

A life of high pace is a life of disturbance. There is an elusive void
within and we do not know what is the cause of it. It will manifest as
anxiety or some low intensity sadness or numbness and apathy. It may
be expressed as disturbance that bursts into wanton violence or,
simply, anti-social abrasiveness. This is the life of the person who,
like a wolf in captivity, is denied of their open vistas, of the wide
field of view that allows each one to stay calm.

In narrowness there is fear. It runs counter to our constitution and
thus triggers in us the feeling of estrangement or disconnect from our
roots. We are not meant to be super fast at all times. In going full
speed we depart from our baseline and in making that our default we
operate beyond the boundaries of the sustainable, hence the
disturbance and the fear of that which remains undetectable.

The slow pace is how we regain control of the situation and
effectively open our conscience to the world that envelops us. Any
action that forces us to reduce our speed is benign. When done with
intent, this will typically be embedded as a ritual. For example, if
we spend 5 minutes praying before each meal, those are 5 minutes that
we have forced ourselves to be patient: what the prayer itself says is
irrelevant to the detunnelling effect that is unfolding.

Rituals with a spiritual significance have the advantage of
establishing in our conscience the notion of necessity. If we think
that we can always skip them, then we introduce cheat days and
eventually come up with all sorts of exceptions that turn into excuses
for not committing to the ritual at all. Whereas when the ritual is
expressed as a matter of spirituality, it becomes non-negotiable. Only
absolute necessity stops you from carrying out what you believe in.

To the disciplined person, such as myself, rituals and the overall
structure for daily life come from within. I personally do not need
anybody to force me to slow down. I am always patient and undisturbed
because my own rules are sacrosanct: I do not have off days in how I
apply them. To everybody else though, religiosity is the best way to
make rituals, and thus detunnelling, happen with regularity. Within a
religious setting, you have fellow practitioners that all commit to
observing the rituals without taking shortcuts. And, if you believe in
the divine, you also recognise that a higher power is involved.

God is everywhere, but to the person with the tunnelled conscience God
does not exist even if they consider themselves religious. Belief as
such is not relevant. One can make the casual claim "I believe in
God", which usually has the same meaning as "I do not believe in
anything in particular". The reason is that belief only matters when
it is expressed as a lifestyle. It otherwise is tokenistic, an
exercise in virtue signalling, or a parroting of precepts that are
devoid of substance.

Belief in God means that you understand how you are part of the whole
and, as such, how the widest possible field of view is the one that
allows you to notice divine presence throughout. Your sense of self
extends beyond the confines of your body to encompass increasingly
more of your extended reality. You are closer to the divine when your
mind is not racing anywhere and, conversely, you turn towards
yourself, effectively deifying your ego, the faster your experience
gets.

Through rituals we can get a glimpse of cosmic oneness by expanding
our conscience. There no longer are indelible lines between the "me"
and the "we", between human and plant, animate and inanimate. All that
is present exists on a continuum. Me, you, those who think they are
great, and those who resent their failures while seeking to become
somebody special, we all are equally partaking of the whole like folds
of the same cloth.

Slowing down puts things in perspective. It empowers us to overcome
petty concerns with their squabbles. It also frames our mind to
understand the symbolism of our deeds, that the rituals and all the
items we rely on are media for our detunnelling. Their value consists
in slowing us down, in making us experience the oneness, but they are
otherwise not worthy of our worship.

You do not need to spend 5 minutes expressing the most eloquent words
ever codified as a prayer, for example. Even mumbling or remaining
silent will suffice. Similarly, you do not require a theology or
social apparatus of power, with high priests and opulent temples, to
notice the vastness that environs you. Whether those structures are
helpful or not will depend on your attitude: to see what they help you
achieve, rather than to treat them as worthwhile in their own right.

The tokenistic mind is that of the person who speaks with impeccable
political correctness and the most class-appropriate accent while
actually expressing contempt for all those it considers inferior. It
also is the mind of the idolater who misunderstands the function of
sanctity at-large. There are no miraculous icons or temples or
mountains or motions of the body or whatnot. All are designed to
instil in us a sense of a world that extends beyond our self; a world
we begin to notice through the openness of the slow-paced life.
