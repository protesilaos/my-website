---
title: "Re: how do you manage your curiosity?"
excerpt: "Excerpt from a private exchange where I comment on how to make best use of one's curiosity."
---

The following is an excerpt from a private exchange. I am sharing it
with permission from my correspondent, without disclosing their identity.
My point is how to make best use of one's curiosity.

* * *

> Now, I would like to ask how you manage your curiosity.
>
> I have questions about almost every subject I encounter, which I
> don't necessarily need to answer at that time, and tend to
> immediately search for answers.
>
> For example, I was once curious about system calls, so I grabbed an
> OS book and read about them. I could've just stopped here, but then
> I was curious about how they actually work in Linux; I wasn't
> satisfied by just knowing the theoretical aspects of it. Eventually,
> I was able to find out how they work at the assembly level. Although
> it was fun, this deep knowledge wasn't actually needed for my
> project.
>
> This also happens to non-CS fields like pure mathematics or science,
> which for me is impossible to gain deep knowledge right away.
>
> In summary, this tendency to go down rabbit holes does more harm
> than good, especially when I have tasks I'd planned to do during the
> day.
>
> So recently, I've been trying to steer myself towards focusing on
> the projects I chose, to achieve my goals in the long term. When I
> have a question, I first ask myself whether it's relevant to my
> current projects, and whether it's okay to seek answers right at
> that time.
>
> However, it's often painful to do so, and it almost feels like a
> punishment for my curiosity. I blame myself whenever I try to
> satisfy my curiosity instead of doing the task. I'm afraid I'll
> gradually kill my curious mind if this continues.
>
> I'd love to hear your opinions on this as well as your experience.

Curiosity has immense potential. Though it cannot realise it without a
structure around it. Generally, talent reaches its finest expression
once it is placed in the right framework. Otherwise, it gets wasted or
falls short of what it could have been.

There has to be a goal for the curious mind. Something greater than
curiosity for the sake of curiosity, which provides immediate guidance
on whether a given path is worth exploring or not.

The goal is like the lodestar. It is a fixed position in the night
sky. You know whether you are moving towards it or are deviating from
it.

The curious mind is akin to the seafarer. Imagine the sailors who hop
on a ship to explore some uncharted waters. Nobody knows what they
will find there. Maybe there is some exotic island, filled with nice
fruit, which has the conditions for a decent collective life. Or maybe
there are untold horrors there that nobody is prepared to deal with.

Those explorers are extra useful to their people when they eventually
make a clearing for some settlement. They will build a port and some
houses, which will gradually grow to become a community. What they
discover thus becomes available to those who are not as exploratory in
nature. The community is the resulting common good which others
partake in.

The curious mind is the mind of the pioneer. Though those who venture
first into the unknown also are the ones who take on the greatest
risk. They are the most likely to suffer whatever pain awaits them.
Curiosity can be disempowering and will then feel like a curse. This
happens when the goal is not clear or, as is usually the case, when
the goal is something the person is not passionate about.

Goals can feel empty when they are not embedded in the conscience as
important. In other words, they are not connected to something greater
still. For example, a student might not understand why it is useful to
study because they have not connected the fruits of studying to some
concrete lifelong benefit. Having sight of the immediate target of the
study is not enough: it has to be contextualised in something that
gives it meaning.

This brings me to the point of purpose. You may find yourself getting
distracted away from your immediate projects. At first, this seems
like an undesirable outcome. Though consider the possibility that your
body is effectively asking "why am I even doing this?". It is
wondering where the lodestar is.

When you go down a rabbit hole, which appears to be pointless, you can
interpret it as a self-harming indulgence, which it might well be, or
you can try to understand whether there is something deeper in you,
something sincere that is being suppressed, which seeks clarity about
your purpose.

Some people have a direct path to their purpose. The curious mind does
not. It explores all the time in what effectively is its way of
understanding itself through its relationship with the world. Think of
the curious mind like the bat which sees itself in a space formed by
echoes.

It is possible to kill curiosity. Sometimes people die inside and are
forever broken, never to find their zest again and make any meaningful
contribution that fulfils them. Though I do not expect this to happen
merely by directing your curiosity. It will have to be something more
injurious.

Now, what does "directing your curiosity" mean in practice? At first,
it means that you have to accept that you may not yet have the
experience to know who you are because you have not figured out where
you are. To reuse the bat metaphor, you need to generate more echo,
which you do by letting your curiosity lead you where it must.

In the second phase though, you have enough experience to know more
about yourself. You can tell with confidence what your strengths are
and how you may harness them. You will have at least an inkling of
what it is you want and which is the figurative haven you will stay
at. You thus learn to channel your curiosity like a laser beam. It is
so concentrated that it is supremely effective in its results.

This is where I come in. I too have a curious mind. This has a purely
intellectual dimension, expressed as inquisitiveness to figuratively
clear the path towards a more complete understanding of a given
subject. Though it also has a physical expression in how I literally
cleared the land where I built my house, for example.

When I was a student at school, I did not understand why studying was
important, so I did not study. I always had the mind I have now, but I
was not directing it in ways that were intellectually inclined. I was
not doing anything noteworthy on that front. Indeed, I was surprised
with myself when I started to realise that I could be a thinker. I was
reading some book and my thoughts were racing in all directions,
trying to compute the implications of what I was learning and then to
connect my findings to other thoughts in what was a network of
sequences that all made sense. It was an explosion of coherent ideas,
even if their initial expression was not as refined.

There was a point where I was reading too much about everything I
could get my hands on. Eventually, I realised that I could not move in
all directions. Over time, the immediate goal I had, which was to keep
the job I got in the field I studied (European Union politics), proved
to be something I was not passionate about. That was a moment of
crisis.

The word "crisis" has the meaning of a difficult situation. Though it
also means "judgement". It is, in other words, an opportunity, however
painful, to critically assess the situation and move forward from a
position of knowledge. I realised that my calling was not to be met in
the world of day-to-day politics. I moved permanently to the
countryside I had lived in before. I have stayed here ever since and
have become a philosopher in the process. Being a philosopher is
something that could not be anticipated by my trajectory as a
teenager: I was not intellectually inclined at all and was merely
interested in football (soccer).

My curiosity became a sharp tool. I directed it at Linux, for example,
and learnt enough to be highly productive on the computer, despite the
fact that I only got my first laptop in my ~mid-twenties. I learnt Git
and the command-line well beyond what was absolutely essential for my
everyday tasks. I then shifted my focus to Emacs and understood it
reasonably well, to the point where I even taught myself how to
program in Lisp, despite not having any formal training in this or
neighbouring fields. Same idea of all the other activities that form
part of my life: the house I now have, dogs, agriculture...

I let my curiosity take me far enough. Though I have a sense of what
is "too far"; a sense that I got through experience. "Experience"
basically means trial and error. You will have to feel some pain to
know when to stop. The body is an excellent mechanism in this regard.
It is why you want to put ideas to the test. Do not keep them in your
head. Try things out, give them a material expression, which will then
allow your body to give you the fine feedback it is capable of. If we
are talking about computer science, write some application, do some
reverse engineering, explore some algorithm. In general, put your
thoughts to the test.

Overthinking is how you waste the potential of the body. You let the
brain test everything without realising that the thought experiment is
not the right tool for reporting feedback at a deep level. You want to
be "out there", living your ideas. This is how the curious mind best
understands itself, acquires the experience it needs, and is then
equipped with the skill of channelling its immense power wherever it
wants.

My curious mind remains curious about everything. Though it is
embodied in a body that has recognised its limits. Concretely, I know
not to go down rabbit holes whose end result I cannot justify. For
example, what will I actually do if I learn about the inner workings
of the Linux kernel? Yes, I do have the intellectual curiosity to seek
an answer to that question, though I know that in practice any answer
is not enough, in isolation, to make me a kernel developer or,
generally, to give me some concrete long-term value.

There are so many topics I would like to learn more about. But because
I have developed the attitude of living my ideas, of embedding them in
everyday conduct, I can anticipate how many otherwise interesting
topics are not viable for me. This makes them not interesting in
practice, although they are interesting in theory.

This is the key then. Continue to experiment and to explore. There
will be ups and downs. You will feel lost at times and wonder whether
there is any meaning to what you are doing. Learn to gradually
integrate your findings into your corpus of knowledge. You do this by
putting ideas to practice.

When something happens in practice, it creates consequences;
consequences you normally have to live with. Where there are enough of
these consequences, they effectively form a framework around your
actions: you cannot do whatever you want because the scope of your
choices has been narrowed by your prior decisions.

For example, if I have 4 hours of free time in the day, I can indulge
my curiosity or commit to something I already have put a lot of effort
into, such as to write an essay for my website, deal with my
correspondence which I greatly enjoy, maintain my Emacs packages, work
my land, play with my dogs...

Which brings forth the issue of caring that I hinted at earlier when I
mentioned how one is passionate about something. For example, I love
my dogs, so spending quality time with them is not a generic entry on
my wishlist, but a major part of my life that I want to safeguard and
continuously nurture. Same idea for my other projects. The website,
for example, has been going since 2011 and I publish long-form,
hand-written text continuously: I do it because I love it. And "I love
it" is something that grew over time, as I kept trying until I
realised that it was something I wanted to live with long-term.

Learn, then, to trust your feelings. Do not just do what reason
dictates or society expects. For example, do not say "I should be
making money, but instead here I am reading about THING". Of course,
money is useful up to a point, but if you are doing it without any
kind of love, then you will burn out. By trusting your feelings more,
you will discover what reverberates in you and you will then be more
likely to recognise it, to commit to it, to understand it in depth,
and to become who you are capable of being.

Remember that the many commitments naturally contain your curiosity,
which is how they may then make you more picky and adept at using your
curiosity in a laser-like kind of way.
